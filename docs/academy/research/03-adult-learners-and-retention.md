## Summary

1. **Adult-specific research is thin and mostly small and qualitative.** There is no large trial of adult group classes or adult retention. The best evidence is a set of small studies plus child and teen dropout research. Design decisions below mix evidence with judgment, and each is labeled.
2. **Adults stay for community, a repertoire they love, and visible progress; they leave when time pressure meets lost confidence.** In a life-history study, practical reasons for stopping (time, energy, health) often masked a deeper loss of confidence or identity. The difference between players who continued and those who lapsed was whether music felt like it added to life or competed with it. [S: Pitts]
3. **Child and teen dropout follows unmet autonomy, competence and relatedness.** Forced repertoire and theory, setbacks, weak teacher or peer bonds, and competing activities drove quitting in a Slovenian focus-group study. In an Austrian survey, parental involvement was the strongest predictor of low dropout tendency. About half of UK and German teens had stopped music by age 17. [S]
4. **Habits take longer than a term.** Lally et al. found about 66 days on average to reach a plateau of automaticity (range 18 to a predicted 254 days), and about half of participants had not reached habit level by day 84. One missed day barely mattered; many missed days did. Gamo's supervised practice session is a built-in cue and should be treated as the habit anchor, not an extra. [S]
5. **Practice quality, spacing and self-regulation matter more than raw hours.** Spacing helps (large meta-analysis of verbal tasks [S-search]), self-regulated-learning instruction in music shows a medium pooled effect (g about 0.56, only 7 studies, caveats) [S], mental practice helps but less than physical practice [S], and interleaving shows only modest, mostly non-significant effects in a tiny sample [S].
6. **Adult classes should be different in kind, not just slower:** small cohorts (4 to 6), a playable real song in session 1, adult repertoire including worship songs and hymns, "just in time" theory, optional low-stakes sharing, and explicit teaching of how to practice. [mostly K; supported by S where cited]
7. **Mixed-age classes work best as events and ensembles, not as the main instrument class.** Parent-and-child works for young children when the parent is trained first (Suzuki model [S-search]). I found no research on grandparent classes, so treat those as an experiment.
8. **Church pathway:** an audition that is "more like an interview," a mentor, a trial period and a "not yet, here is training" answer are common church practice [S]. For tech roles, a practitioner source expects 6 to 12 months to a solo weekend service and says to train midweek, not on Sunday [S]. Gamo can offer a staged pathway with skill gates; the church keeps the call on readiness to serve.

## 1. How adult classes should differ from children's classes

### 1.1 What the evidence says about adults as learners

- **Andragogy (Knowles).** Adults want to know why they are learning something, expect some self-direction, bring prior experience, and prefer problem-centered learning tied to real life. Sources agree on the list but differ on its history and I could not read Knowles directly. [S-search: https://pce.sandiego.edu/what-is-andragogy/] For Gamo this translates to: say why each skill matters for the songs they want to play, let them choose songs, and use their experience.
- **Goals are clear from the start.** A 711-student survey of adult piano students in 24 U.S. states rated skill benefits highest, with play/fun, accomplishment, technique and challenge top in importance; social and cultural benefits were rated higher than in earlier studies. [S-search: Jutras 2006]
- **Self-regulation loop.** MacIntyre and colleagues proposed a loop in adult musicians: desire to learn leads to effort, effort builds perceived competence, competence builds desire. [S-search]
- **Slow progress and embarrassment.** Adult piano learners get frustrated quickly if competence does not arrive soon, and some feel embarrassed to be adult beginners. [S-search: small surveys; weak]
- **Slower, not less able.** Older adults progress more slowly on cognitive and psychomotor aspects but can acquire instrumental skills; teaching adapted to older learners can reduce frustration. [S-search: Reifinger 2016 abstract summary]
- **Group cohesion varies.** In a 10-week piano program for 17 older novices (mean age 70.9), groups of up to 4 met for 60-minute weekly lessons with 30 minutes of recommended daily home practice. Participants valued ensemble playing and liked repertoire they knew; motivation was highest when everyone liked the repertoire and the group bonded. Groups were also a source of anxiety and irritation, especially unison playing when partners were out of time. Classes differed clearly in cohesion. The study was small and results mixed. [S: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6968490]
- **Adults want adult repertoire.** Same study: participants wanted material different from children's music. Some struggled with pieces combining chords and melody. [S]

### 1.2 Recommended ideal structure (adult classes)

Add an assistant (a trained senior student or volunteer teacher) at 6. The 30-minute class length in the brief is better suited to children; keep 30 minutes only for a short "lab" format. |

Church venues: avoid clashing with midweek services. | [K]. Time is the most commonly reported barrier for adults [S-search; Pitts 2017 life histories confirm time/energy as stated reasons [S]]. |
| Pacing | One song path, not a method book path. Session 1 ends with the group playing a recognizable song (two to three chords, simple pattern). New skill every week, with built-in review. Adult repertoire, not "kiddie" arrangements. | Familiar and liked repertoire boosted motivation in older novices [S]. Choice of repertoire is a recommendation of the Slovenian authors [S]. | Social format | Fixed cohort of the same people for a 10 to 12 week term; first session includes introductions and everyone, including the teacher, playing one short thing; a "buddy" for practice; a shared group chat or email list moderated by staff. | Group music-making was the biggest motivator in adult interviews [S-search: Music Teacher magazine]. In a 10-week adult piano performance group, the first-session rite of everyone playing "dissipated" tension [S]. |
| Performance anxiety | Make sharing **low stakes and optional**: play for the class from week 3, group (not solo) first, applause only, no comparison, no grading. A mid-term "open room" and an end-of-term informal showcase with a printed program. | In the adult piano group: non-competitive (nobody playing the same piece), "low embarrassment"; all but two of 8 reported more performance confidence; the main negatives were dread before the first session and two hours of performing being too long [S]. All [K]-level practice advice, not clinical guidance. |

### 1.3 Three adult tracks

**Track A: Absolute beginner (default).**

- Term: 12 weeks, 60 minutes class plus 60 minutes supervised practice.
- Goal by week 4: play and sing or play along with one full song at slow tempo.
- Goal by week 12: three songs, one performed for the class.
- Placement: simple intake form (instrument, goals, songs they love, physical notes). No audition.

**Track B: Returning player ("violin in the attic").**

- Many lapsed players' real barrier is confidence and the sense of being judged, not skill. Practical reasons such as time and energy often mask a crisis of confidence or identity. [S: Pitts 2017]
- Offer a 20-minute placement chat plus a "play me something" with no pressure, then place in a Returners cohort (same adult beginner structure, faster first 4 weeks, more repertoire and band playing).
- Explicitly name the rust: "most returners need 4 to 8 weeks to feel like themselves again." [K, unverified]. Do not compare returners with absolute beginners in the same group.
- Offer a short 6-week refresher option for people who cannot commit to a full term. (A six-lesson option is used by at least one community school [S-search: Bloomingdale].)

**Track C: Church-volunteer fast track (play on Sunday).**

- Purpose: a defined short path to playing a small set of songs for a congregation, with honest limits.
- Structure (proposal, [K]): 8 weeks, 60 minutes class plus 60 minutes supervised practice, then 4 band rehearsals with a partner-church leader.
- Content: 6 to 8 songs the partner church actually plays, in the church's keys; Nashville number charts; simple roles (strum or fingerpick patterns, bass root notes, pad and block chords on keys, cajon or simple kit patterns, unison or harmony vocal line); playing with a click or track; stopping and starting together; following a leader.
- Keys: congregational comfort range is often cited around B-flat3 to D5 for melodies, and songs pitched too high cause people to stop singing. These are practitioner rules of thumb, not measured research. [S-search: Worship Leader and others; no peer-reviewed measurement found]
- Honest framing: the fast track gets someone ready for a trial Sunday role with supervision, not general musicianship. After the term they can continue to Track A or B content.

### 1.4 Repertoire notes for worship songs and hymns

- Teach worship songs from lead sheets and Nashville numbers so students can change keys. [K]
- Hymns teach part-writing and reading and give a shared repertoire across generations. They are slower to start with for guitar beginners, so use them in the second half of a term. [K]
- Each term: 60 percent songs the group chooses, 40 percent songs in the partner church's current set. [K]
- Licensing: any use of copyrighted lyrics or charts needs checking (for example through the church's existing license). This is a compliance item to confirm before launch. [K]

## 2. Mixed-age and family classes

### What the evidence supports

- **Home and parent support predict persistence for children.** A musical home environment was the strongest predictor of staying musically active in a UK and German longitudinal sample (cross-sectional beta -0.71, mixed model beta -0.57); starting age was not significant [S: PMC8612519]. In a 140-student Austrian survey, perceived parental involvement had the strongest path to lower dropout tendency (beta -0.39) [S: Frontiers 2024]. Both are correlational.
- **Parents need preparation.** In Suzuki programs parents typically take lessons or training first, attend all lessons and gradually step back as the child grows [S-search: macphail.org]. Keeping a child motivated at home was the most common challenge for Suzuki parents in one survey [S-search]. A small interview study of six U.S. parents found they struggled in the "home teacher" role [S-search].
- **Autonomy matters for older children and teens.** In the Slovenian study, parent-initiated enrollment and limited repertoire choice were key to lack of autonomy, a leading internal reason for dropping out [S]. Forced joint classes risk the same problem.
- **Family music programs help families, with weak evidence.** A study of integrated parent-child music classes reported positive caregiver experiences and stronger parent-child bonds (interviews, not outcomes data). [S-search: Lense et al. 2020]
- **Grandparents and intergenerational classes.** I found no study of grandparent participation in instrument classes. One intergenerational music-therapy trial exists but I did not see its results. [S-search] Treat as unevidenced.

### When mixed-age works (recommendations, [K] unless cited)

| Format | Works when | Avoid when |
|---|---|---|
| Parent-and-child instrument class (ages 5 to 8) | The parent attends every class and learns the basics first; parent role is "practice coach and fellow beginner," not teacher; step back by age 9 to 10. | The child is 10+ or a teen who wants independence; the parent is a reluctant attendee. |
| Family ensemble or "Family Jam Night" (all ages, monthly, one hour) | Songs are simple and shared (hymns, worship songs, folk), each person plays at their level, parts are tiered (easy, medium, harder), and everyone gets a role. | Anyone must play alone in front of relatives without choice. |
| Adult and teen in same class | Both are real beginners at the same level and both chose it. | Teens feel observed by parents; adults feel judged by teens. A common adult worry is being judged [S: Pitts]. |
| Grandparent and grandchild | As an event or short workshop (for example, a two-hour "Learn a hymn together"), not a term-long class, until there is local evidence. | As a core pathway. |

Rule: **group by level and goal, not by age, for instrument classes; mix ages for ensembles and events.** [K]

## 3. Retention

### 3.1 Why students quit

**Children and teens**

- **Slovenia (focus groups, 29 participants: 6 dropout students aged about 10 to 13, parents, teachers, principals).** Internal factors: lack of autonomy (parent-initiated enrollment, limited repertoire), competence concerns (setbacks in competitions and exams, comparisons with siblings), weak teacher bonds and little peer interaction, and individual factors including stage fright. External: competing extracurricular commitments (23 mentions), required music theory and solfege (18), teacher approach, social environment, resources, workload. Authors framed this with self-determination theory. The Statistical Office figures imply about 86.5 percent dropout from first-year instrumental programs, but the authors say this is tentative because no cohort data exist. [S: https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1385840/full]
- **Austria (140 students aged 8 to 27, mean 12.9).** Intrinsic motivation correlated with lower dropout tendency (r = -0.50), parental involvement had the strongest path (beta -0.39), controlled motivation predicted higher dropout (beta 0.19). Dropout tendency was one self-report item and the design was cross-sectional. [S: https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1378843/full]
- **UK and Germany (3,303 students, ages 10 to 17).** Share still musically active by age: 10 = 100 percent, 14 = 91, 15 = 86, 16 = 72, 17 = 49. Hazard peaks at 17 (0.32) then 16 (0.16). Strongest predictor was a musical home environment; belief that musicality can improve with practice and conscientiousness were smaller predictors. The study did not measure reasons for quitting. [S: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8612519]
- **McPherson and Davidson** studied 157 beginning instrumentalists aged 7 to 9 and compared those who stopped with those who continued over the first year; a longitudinal study of 160 Australian children found parental support is high early and tapers [S-search: University of Melbourne records]. I did not see the dropout-month results.
- **Older US data.** A 1981 Kansas City study found a 22 percent instrumental dropout rate and that 32.6 percent of dropouts had never been recruited by the building instructor [S-search; dated].

**Adults**

- **Life-history study of lapsed and continuing amateur players (Pitts).** Life transitions (leaving home, a new job, children, ill health, retirement) influenced joining or leaving. The main difference was whether music enhanced or conflicted with other life demands. Reported challenges: finding time to practice, feeling "judged" by other players, lacking confidence, struggling to keep up in rehearsals. Those joining for social reasons were sometimes disappointed. Where players feel supported and valued, playing buffers stress; where they feel insecure, withdrawal can feel necessary. [S: https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/110520/1/Violin%20in%20the%20Attic%20M%26L%20Handbook%20chapter.pdf]
- **Adult piano group (8 amateurs, 10 weeks).** The most disliked aspect of playing was not improving or repeating errors, and frustration over time. [S: https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/103313/1/RSME_Piano_performance_Revised.docx]
- **Teacher interviews** report adults need routine and feedback to keep momentum. [S-search: Music Teacher magazine; blocked when opened]

### 3.2 When do students quit? (dropout months)

- **No rigorous month-by-month data for adult instrument classes was found.** I found no peer-reviewed retention curve for adults in community music classes.
- Child and teen data exist only by age: sharp rise in quitting at ages 15 to 17 [S].
- A vendor blog title claims music schools lose "8 to 10 percent of students every month," but I did not open it and it is a software vendor (unverified). Do not use it as a planning figure.
- **Recommendation:** Gamo should measure its own drop-off from day one: attendance and practice-session check-ins at weeks 2, 4, 6, 8 and 12, and the number of students who miss two sessions in a row. [K]

| 14 | **Competitions, ranks, public leaderboards** | Counter-indicated | Low | Setbacks in competitions and exams and sibling comparisons were reasons for quitting [S]. Not recommended for the adult program. |

Do not use "free trial then paid" funnel language in design. Retention is about service reliability and mission impact, not conversion. Any fee or contribution tiers remain proposals pending owner approval.

## 4. Practice habits and accountability

### 4.1 Frequency and duration

- **There is no peer-reviewed prescription for adult beginners' practice time.** Practitioner sources suggest roughly 15 to 30 minutes a day, at least 5 days a week, with consistency valued over length; adults must learn to structure their own practice and tie it to an existing routine. [S-search: music-school blogs; weak]
- Older novices in the one trial were asked for 30 minutes a day. [S: PMC6968490]
- **Total time is a poor guide on its own.** In a diary study, formal practice (scales, pieces, technical work) tracked achievement while total practice time did not [S-search: Sloboda et al. 1996]. In a small study of 17 advanced pianists, practice accuracy predicted next-day performance better than practice duration (correlational; one session; next-day test only) [S-search: Duke et al. 2009].
- **Deliberate practice** accounts for a meaningful but limited share of performance differences: a meta-analysis of 88 studies reported an average correlation of .35 (about 12 percent of variance); a music-specific reanalysis found r = 0.44 [S-search: Macnamara et al. 2014; Platz et al.]. Practice matters; it is not the whole story.

**Recommended guideline for Gamo adults (proposal, [K]):** 20 minutes on 5 days per week at home (100 minutes per week) plus the supervised hour, aiming for a total of about 3 hours per week. Accept 10 minutes on a hard day as a win. Show that missing one day does not break the habit (Lally).

### 4.2 Habit formation

Lally et al. (2010): 96 undergraduates (82 completed) chose a daily behavior with a cue and logged it for 84 days. About 66 days on average to reach maximum automaticity (individual range 18 to a predicted 254), early repetitions produced the largest gains, then a plateau. One missed day had little effect; many missed days lowered the maximum automaticity reached. About half had not reached habit level by day 84; exercise took about 1.5 times as long as eating or drinking behaviors. Self-reported automaticity. [S: https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-form-habit]

Implications:

- A 10 to 12 week term is shorter than the average habit-formation window. Offer a 24-week "first year" arc and tell students it takes months for practice to feel automatic.
- Cue plus context: practice at the same time and place, attached to something that already happens ("after dinner dishes").
- Do not shame missed days; show the recovery rule: never miss twice. ([K], a coaching rule consistent with the Lally missed-day finding.)

### 4.3 Supervised practice sessions ("office hours", practice labs, practice clubs)

- **What I could not find:** a controlled study showing that supervised practice labs improve adult habit formation or retention. [Gap]
- **Reasoning, [K] built on S-sources:** (1) The practice hour is a fixed cue in a fixed context, which is what Lally's design used. (2) It reduces the "finding time" barrier named by adults [S: Pitts]. (3) It lets a supervisor catch errors and teach strategies that SRL research says students seldom get from the lesson alone [S: PMC5006062]. (4) It makes practice social, which addresses isolation.
- **Accountability evidence is weak and secondary.** A widely cited Dominican University study (267 recruited, 149 completed) reported that people who wrote goals, shared them with a friend and sent weekly updates reported 76 percent goal progress against 43 percent for unwritten goals; ratings were self-reported and secondary summaries describe the measure inconsistently. [S-search: https://canr.msu.edu/news/achieving_your_goals_an_evidence_based_approach; treat as suggestive only]
- **Recommended formats:**
  - **Practice lab (default):** 60 minutes right after class, supervised by a teacher or trained assistant at 1 to 8 ratio, with rooms or headphones for quiet practice.
  - **Practice club:** open evening, one extra supervised hour per week, drop-in, buddy pairs.
  - **Office hours:** 15-minute slots for one-to-one help, booked in the lab hour.
  - Supervisors coach strategy, not just "play it again."

### 4.4 Spacing, interleaving, mental practice

- **Spacing.** Cepeda et al. (2006) reviewed 839 assessments in 317 experiments in 184 articles of distributed practice (verbal recall tasks): the best gap between sessions increased with the delay until the final test. [S-search: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/] The follow-up (2008) tested 1,350+ people with gaps up to 3.5 months and tests up to a year: the optimal gap grew with test delay, about 20 percent of the delay at a few weeks falling to about 5 percent at a year; one excerpt gave different ratios, so quote neither (unverified). [S-search: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/] The tasks were verbal, not motor or music. Practical read-across ([K]): spread practice across 5 days rather than one long session, and revisit older songs weekly.
- **Interleaving.** 10 advanced clarinetists (mostly aged 19 to 22) practiced two pieces blocked and two interleaved (12 minutes per piece): interleaved scored consistently higher, mostly not significantly; raters disagreed; most preferred blocked practice even though they found interleaved more useful. Authors call findings modest and promising. [S: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4989027] Use in labs as a mix: alternate between two or three songs and a technique drill every few minutes, but do not over-promise.
- **Mental practice.** 16 expert pianists (at least 9 years of instruction), 7 minutes of practice at a time: mental practice reduced wrong notes but less than physical practice; both produced movement anticipation; only physical practice reduced variability; small sample. [S: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3747442] Music and sport reviews suggest combining mental and physical practice can approach extended physical practice [S-search]. Use for 2 to 3 minutes per lab: imagine and finger the next phrase before playing.
- **Self-regulation instruction.** Pooled g about 0.56 across 7 studies, inconsistent reporting, no adult-specific evidence. [S: PMC12734040]. A study of novice adult string players tested a self-regulation worksheet; I did not see its results [S-search].

## 5. Pathway: adults to church worship teams and tech teams

### 5.1 What churches do (evidence level: practitioner, not research)

- **Audition as interview.** One church training document describes the process as "not a standard audition, but more like an interview," to know the musical gifts in the church, give members an accurate view of their gifts, and spot training needs. It lists what to listen for by instrument (for example for guitar: strum, consistency of tempo, fills; for drums: steady tempo, following a leader in the rise and fall of songs) and outcomes such as "you are called," "you are called but we have no position now," and "get more training, or come back for an audition later." It also advises checking that the person is in a good place spiritually before replying and providing ongoing accountability, training and pastoral care. [S: https://sovereigngracemusic.s3.amazonaws.com/wp-content/uploads/20230821180852/auditioning_and_developing_musicians-wg06-kboer.pdf]
- **Trial period with a mentor, same instrument where possible; re-audition yearly; background checks where children are present.** Church web pages describe this in many forms; no standard hours exist. [S-search: mercyhillchurch.com, worshiponline.com, others]
- **Redirect, don't reject.** Candidates who are not ready are often offered other service roles or a path to develop. [S-search]
- **RSCM Voice for Life** is a graded training scheme for church singers with levels, modules (vocal skills, theory, repertoire, aural, choir in context) and informal assessment by the choir director. It is an example of a defined structure, not research. [S-search: https://www.rscm.org.uk/learn-with-us/vfl-digital/]
- **Tech teams.** A veteran sound-training article says not to expect solo front-of-house operation in under 6 months, and most need 6 to 12 months to be ready for a weekend; use milestones, not dates; start with the stage (mic locker, cables, stage plot) before the console; do not train on weekends; train during the week with a virtual soundcheck; avoid "stand behind the mixer" shadowing during live services; not everyone will run the weekend, but they can be valuable as assistants or midweek operators. [S: https://www.prosoundweb.com/church-sound-training-audio-volunteers/] Another source sketches a three-week observe, set up, run-with-supervisor model [S-search: https://www.whatstheprocessfor.com/blog/church-sound-booth-training]. No industry-standard hour count exists.

### 5.2 Proposed Gamo pathway (design, [K])

**Principle:** Gamo certifies skills and readiness to audition. The partner church decides on serving, including spiritual and character readiness, background checks and Sunday placement. Gamo never promises a place, and church partnership or payment never changes vetting or rankings.

**Worship team: instrument and voice**

| Gate | What the student does | Approximate hours |
|---|---|---|
| 0. Start | Track A or B placement | 0 |
| 1. Foundations | One 12-week term (60 min class plus 60 min supervised practice), plus about 20 min a day at home | about 24 supervised plus about 20 home |
| 2. Band skills | A second term, or the 8-week fast track, in a band format: play with click or track, read Nashville charts, start/stop together, follow a leader, play two or three roles | about 16 to 24 supervised |
| 3. Observe | Attend 2 team rehearsals as an observer and 1 Sunday | about 6 |
| 4. Trial | Play 3 to 4 rehearsals with the partner team and a mentor (same instrument if possible), interview with the music leader | about 8 to 10 |
| 5. Supervised Sundays | 3 services with the mentor present, in a limited role (for example 2 songs) | about 6 |
| 6. Ongoing | Monthly team rehearsal plus an annual check-in; return to Gamo practice lab at will | ongoing |

Rough total before first independent Sunday: **about 50 to 70 supervised hours for the standard path, about 35 to 40 for the fast track** (fast track: one 8-week term plus 4 rehearsals plus 3 supervised Sundays, limited to a small set of songs). These are proposals, not research results.

**Tech team (sound, slides and livestream)**

| Gate | What the student does | Approximate hours |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Stage | Mic locker, cables, stage plot, set the stage from an input sheet; safety | about 8 |
| 2. Signal flow and console basics | Weekday sessions on a console with a virtual soundcheck on recorded material | about 16 |
| 3. Observe | Sit with the operator for 3 services (listening, asking, no live responsibility yet) | about 9 |
| 4. Run with a mentor | Run setup and mix portions of 3 services with the mentor next to them | about 9 |
| 5. Solo, supervised | First solo service with the mentor available | variable |

About 40 hours and at least 3 months of elapsed time to supervised solo; full weekend readiness may take 6 to 12 months per the practitioner source. Slides and livestream roles can be shorter. [S for the 6 to 12 month claim; rest K]

## Trade-offs when constrained

- **No dedicated practice room or instruments:** run the practice lab in the same room with headphones (keyboards), practice pads and unplugged electric guitars; the drum kit is rotated. Quiet practice is possible; accept lower quality for loud instruments.
- **One teacher, bigger groups:** cap at 8 only with an assistant; below that, keep 6. Drums and voice suffer most from large groups.
- **Cannot offer both class and practice hour:** keep the practice hour and shorten the class to 45 minutes. The fixed practice cue is the part with the strongest habit logic.
- **Only one weekly slot:** pick weekday evening; use Saturday morning once demand justifies it.
- **No volunteer assistants:** drop to 4 to 5 students and extend the practice hour supervision by video review.
- **Short term only (6 weeks):** accept that habit formation will not complete; focus on a first song and an easy path to re-enroll.
- **Small groups (2 to 3 students):** keep the same structure and emphasize the buddy system.
- **Mixed levels in one class:** use tiered parts (A: easy, B: medium, C: harder) of the same song so everyone plays together.

## Implications for Gamo Academy (concrete recommendations)

1. **Class structure.** 4 to 6 adults per teacher, up to 8 only with an assistant. 60-minute class followed by a 60-minute supervised practice lab, same day, same room, same cohort, for 12 weeks. Weekday evening 6:30 to 8:30 pm and Saturday mornings.
2. **Week-1 promise.** Every adult plays a full, recognizable song in the first session. Publish this as an Academy standard.
3. **Repertoire.** 12 to 20 vetted songs per instrument, with the first 4 weeks built around 3-chord worship songs and hymns, each available in two keys; student choice of one song per month. Confirm lyric and chart licensing with a partner church.
4. **Theory.** About 20 percent of class time, just in time, never a separate mandatory class for adults.
5. **Three adult tracks:** Beginner (12 weeks), Returner (12 weeks with a 20-minute placement chat), and the Church Fast Track (8 weeks plus 4 rehearsals). Add an optional 6-week refresher.
6. **Low-stakes sharing.** Optional group play from week 3; "open room" at week 6; an informal showcase at week 12 with a printed program. No grading.
7. **Learn-to-learn.** The first 4 weeks include a teachable 5-step practice routine and error-stopping habit, taught by the teacher and repeated in the lab.
8. **Practice target.** 20 minutes on 5 days per week at home plus the supervised hour. Run a "never miss twice" rule and a pause-not-quit option. Tell students it takes months, not weeks, for practice to feel automatic.
9. **Retention measurement.** Track attendance and lab check-ins at weeks 2, 4, 6, 8 and 12, plus the share of students who miss two sessions in a row. Teacher outreach within 7 days of a miss. Use this to replace guesses with Gamo's own data by the end of the first year.
10. **Retention tactics, in order:** repertoire choice, fixed cohort and buddy, week-1 song, supervised lab, optional sharing, visible skills checklist, written practice plan, growth-mindset messaging, personal outreach.
11. **Mixed-age.** Keep instrument classes by level and goal. Offer a monthly one-hour "Family Jam" with tiered parts for all ages. Run parent-and-child classes only for ages 5 to 8 with a parent orientation first. Treat grandparent workshops as an experiment (two hours, once a term) and track feedback.
12. **Physical access.** Intake question on hands, hearing and vision; chairs with backs, good light, large-print charts, short-scale and light-gauge guitars, hearing-friendly volume, hand warm-ups in every class.
13. **Pathway.** Offer the Worship (about 50 to 70 supervised hours, or 35 to 40 on the fast track) and Tech (about 40 hours over at least 3 months) paths with gates, mentors and weekday-only training. Gamo issues a skills-ready letter; the church decides on serving.
14. **Mission guardrails.** Church partnership, sponsorship or payment never changes a student's evaluation or ranking. No competition or ranking among adult students.

## Open questions for the owner

1. **Class length and Sunday timing.** Should the adult default be 60 minutes plus a 60-minute practice lab (2-hour block), or keep 30 to 45 minutes where venues limit time?
2. **Partner church role.** Will each partner church own the audition, mentor assignment and final yes, with Gamo only certifying skills? Which church leaders will sign the readiness criteria?
3. **Track C boundaries.** Are you comfortable with a short fast track (8 weeks) that prepares a limited set of songs, with a clear message that it is not general musicianship?
4. **Repertoire and licensing.** Who clears the use of worship song lyrics and charts (church licenses, Gamo purchase, or songs in the public domain only)?
5. **Hymns and denominations.** Which hymn tradition(s) and languages should the vetted list include for the first cities?
6. **Assistant teachers.** Are advanced students allowed to serve as lab assistants (training, background checks, any stipend)? This affects the ratio of 1 to 6 and any pay questions, which need your approval.
7. **Family classes.** Is a parent-and-child class in scope for the first year, or only events?
8. **Data and privacy.** Is the owner comfortable with attendance and lab check-in data (by email identity, no accounts, per the current rules) being used for outreach, and what is the retention period?
9. **Physical accommodations.** Which venues can provide chairs, lighting and quiet practice space, and who pays for adaptive gear (short-scale guitars, hearing aids support)?
10. **Terms and re-enrollment.** Do you prefer rolling start dates or fixed 12-week terms with a re-enrollment week? (No fee decisions are implied here.)
