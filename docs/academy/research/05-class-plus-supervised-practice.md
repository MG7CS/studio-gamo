# Research 05: Class plus supervised practice

Gamo Academy research series. Written 2026-11-16. Scope: the weekly shape "a 30-60 minute class, then about one hour of supervised practice at the same site, with the family member waiting or staying." Ideal setup first; limits are collected at the end.

Evidence labels used throughout:

- **[S]** read in a page or document I opened in this session.
- **[S~]** seen only in a web-search result summary this session (I did not open the full source). Treat as a pointer to verify before quoting publicly.
- **[K]** prior knowledge or my own judgment, not verified in a source. Anything numeric marked **(unverified)** or **(arithmetic)** if I computed it.

No statistic or quote below is invented. Where research does not exist, I say so.

---

## Summary

1. **The class-then-practice block is sound, but the research is indirect.** I found no study that directly compares "practice right after the lesson" with "practice later." What exists is consistent: immediate informative feedback and supervision are named conditions of the most effective practice [S, Ericsson summary]; motor skills keep consolidating for hours afterward and with sleep [S~, S]; and replaying the target material before stopping may protect overnight gains [S~, Allen]. A supervised hour right after class fits all of this. It must not be the only practice of the week.
2. **"One hour" should mean one hour in the room, not one hour of continuous playing.** Typical beginners practice about 16-23 minutes a day when they practice [S, Hallam, arithmetic from weekly means]. Attention-span-by-age rules are folk guidance, not research [S~]. Recommended active playing inside the hour: about 20-25 minutes (ages 6-8), 30-35 (9-12), 40-45 (13-17), 45-50 (adults) (all **[K]**), split into short blocks with breaks, a peer round, a log and a parent handoff.
3. **Mentors do check-ins, not teaching.** The lead teacher teaches new material in class; the practice mentor keeps the student on the teacher's goals, asks questions, models the practice routine and reports back. Recommended ratio **[K]**: 1 mentor to 6 students as the default, 1:4 for beginners on strings, winds and brass in the first 8 weeks, up to 1:8 for headphone keyboards or guitar with a teacher console or older students. A mentor makes a 2-3 minute check-in round every 12 minutes.
4. **Sound and hearing are the hard design problem.** University practice rooms averaged 87-95 dB(A), brass highest, and an estimated 48% of students exceeded the allowable daily dose [S~]. NIOSH: 85 dBA for 8 hours, halving the time for every 3 dB [S]. Twelve acoustic beginners in one hall add about 11 dB over one player (arithmetic **[K]**). The ideal is quiet-by-design instruments (digital pianos and electric guitars on headphones, practice pads, electronic kits) plus separate rooms for loud acoustic instruments and voices.
5. **Parents are part of the design, not a waiting-room afterthought.** The Suzuki model, the most developed parent-practice model, has a parent attend every lesson, take notes and act as home teacher [S]; El Sistema programs include family learning and parent ensembles [S]. The practice hour should include a parent corner, a 10-minute weekly "how to support practice at home" mini-session, and a 3-minute handoff at the end.
6. **Tracking can be low-tech and still strong.** A paper goal-and-log card in the student's hand, a one-line weekly email to the family, and a staff form for mentors. Explicit goal-setting, monitoring and evaluation are the best-supported practice skills to teach (metacognition and self-regulation: about +7 months in the EEF toolkit [S~]).
7. **Scale by waves of 12.** One class cohort of 12 plus one practice zone is the repeatable unit. A staggered schedule lets 24 students use one classroom and one practice hall between 9:00 and 12:15, and 48 students use two classrooms and a practice zone for 24 in the same window (tables in section 7).
8. **Practice evolves.** Fully guided for 8 weeks, then student-set goals, then drop-in practice for graduates, with graduates becoming mentors-in-training. Youth safeguarding (two screened, unrelated adults; under-18 mentors never counted as adults) is non-negotiable in a church or school host.

## 1. Is class-then-practice effective? Sequencing, length and breaks

### 1.1 What the evidence says

**Deliberate practice needs supervision and feedback.** The Ericsson, Krampe and Tesch-Romer account of expert practice lists, as conditions: motivation to attend and exert effort, immediate informative feedback and knowledge of results, repeated performance of the same or similar tasks, and "individualized supervision by a teacher is superior" [S, secondary summary at jsomers.net]. A supervised practice hour is the cheapest way to deliver feedback and supervision more than once a week. Caveat: the original study used retrospective practice estimates from a small, selected group of violin students [S~].

**Session length for experts is long; for children it is short.** The same summary says top performers practice about 4 hours a day in 80-120 minute sessions, with "decreasing marginal returns after the first hour and a half of a session," and that duration and frequency "gradually increase" over years [S]. That is an expert ceiling, not a child's starting point. Shinichi Suzuki's own guidance, in the Music Institute of Chicago parent handbook: "At home two or three minutes of practice may be enough for a beginner. Perhaps this can be done four or five times a day," with periods lengthening as concentration grows [S].

**What young musicians actually practice.** In a UK questionnaire study of 3,325 young classical players (self-reported, mostly UK), mean weekly practice by level was [S, Hallam et al., Table 2]:

| Level | Mean minutes per week | Roughly per day if daily (arithmetic) |
|---|---|---|
| Preliminary (beginner), n=370 | 160 (SD 254) | 23 |
| Grade 1, n=262 | 116 | 17 |
| Grade 3, n=217 | 140 | 20 |
| Grade 5, n=442 | 226 | 32 |
| Grade 8, n=330 | 427 | 61 |

By instrument at the beginner (Preliminary) level: keyboard 213, strings 190, brass 111, woodwind 92 minutes per week [S]. The paper found keyboard players practice the most, then strings, brass, woodwind, and few differences in strategy quality [S]. Standard deviations are huge, so these are descriptions of what happens, not targets.

**Attention limits by age.** The popular rule (about 2-3 minutes of attention per year of age) is folk guidance that appears on parenting sites, not in peer-reviewed norms [S~]. An Edutopia review notes sustained-attention studies usually measure limits of 8-25 minutes, rarely above 30 [S~]. Suzuki's handbook itself tells parents to expect "a 5 to 10 minute attention span and sometimes only 5 to 10 seconds" in young children [S]. I use "work in 8-20 minute blocks with breaks" as a design rule **[K]**, not as a research finding.

**Sequencing: practice right after class vs later.**

- No direct comparison study found. A search for one returned only consolidation and interference studies [S~].
- Motor-skill areas of the brain stay active for about 4-6 hours after practice, a window described as stabilization, with sleep as enhancement [S~, summary of motor-learning studies].
- Musicians and sleep: in a 2x2 study (n=40; groups of 9-11), the sleep group improved more than the wake group (21.8% vs 12.4%), musicians improved more than non-musicians (23.7% vs 11.0%), and musicians who stayed awake improved about as much as non-musicians who slept [S, PMC4966959]. Small sample, short lab task.
- Interference: in a study of music majors, learning a second similar melody right after the first erased the first melody's overnight gains; replaying the first melody before stopping seemed to restore them [S~, Allen study, about 60 students]. Authors said it was too early to claim a teaching implication.
- Spacing: one piano study found fewer errors when learning was separated by 24 hours than by 5 minutes or 6 hours; a review said no data allow a firm conclusion about spacing and music skill retention [S~].
- Interleaving: ten clarinetists (mostly aged 19-22) practiced two passages blocked (12 min then 12 min) or interleaved (3-minute alternation). Interleaved scored higher on average in every comparison but rarely significantly and raters disagreed. Eight of ten said interleaving helped goal setting, focus and mistake identification [S, PMC4989027].

### 1.2 What I conclude (ideal design)

All of the following is judgment **[K]** built on the evidence above:

1. **Do practice right after class, while the memory is fresh and a mentor can correct errors on the spot.** The risk of practicing wrong (a Suzuki teacher's point that practicing incorrectly is worse than not practicing [S]) is lowest when feedback is immediate.
2. **Practice the same material the class just covered, and finish by replaying the single most important passage** (the reactivation effect). Do not start a second, similar piece late in the hour.
3. **Add a second touch within 24 hours** (10 minutes at home the next day) so that class-day learning gets a spaced repetition after sleep. The supervised hour is one of about five or six weekly practices, not the whole week.
4. **Keep blocks short and mixed.** Two or three short blocks, alternating a technical item and a piece, is consistent with the interleaving results and with attention limits.
5. **Treat "an hour" as the container.** Warm-up, goal check, two or three playing blocks, a break, a peer round, a log and a handoff all fit in 60 minutes (blueprint, section 9).

### 1.3 Recommended active-playing time by age band and instrument

All figures **[K] (unverified)**, anchored on Suzuki's "grow with concentration" principle [S] and the Hallam daily-equivalent means [S]. Total time in the room stays about 60 minutes for every band so that families keep one schedule; what changes is how much of it is continuous playing.

| Age band | Playing blocks | Total active playing | Rest of the hour |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6-8 | 3 x 8-10 min | 20-25 min | Movement break, listening game, peer round, log with stickers, parent show-and-tell |
| 9-12 | 3 x 10-12 min | 30-35 min | Break, peer round, log |
| 13-17 | 2-3 x 15 min | 40-45 min | Short break, recording and self-review, log |
| Adults | 2-3 x 15-20 min | 45-50 min | Short break, recording, log |

By instrument **[K]**:

- **Keyboard, guitar:** longest blocks acceptable; watch posture and wrists.
- **Strings:** well-fitted instrument size matters (Suzuki handbook requires properly sized instruments and footstools [S]); frequent posture resets for children.
- **Brass and winds:** shorter blocks (about 10 minutes on, 3-5 off) for lip and breath fatigue.
- **Drums and percussion:** pads or electronic kits only in the shared practice hour; blocks of 10-15 minutes with wrist breaks.

## 2. Structure of the supervised session and the mentor role

### 2.1 Session structure (60 minutes)

The full minute-by-minute blueprint is in section 9. Components and the reason for each:

| Component | Purpose | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Arrival and settle (5 min) | Instrument out, tune, find the goal card | [K] |
| Goal-setting card (inside the first 5-10 min) | The teacher's class takeaway becomes today's plan: planning is the first phase of self-regulated practice | Planning, performance, evaluation cycle [S~, McPherson and Zimmerman]; explicit strategy teaching [S~, EEF] |
| Warm-up (5 min) | Physical readiness, known material first (Suzuki sessions open with review, "psychologically beneficial to begin with what one knows well") | [S, Suzuki handbook] |
| Practice blocks (2-3, with mentor check-ins about every 12 min) | The core work, with immediate informative feedback | [S, Ericsson summary] |
| Short break | Rest and attention reset; lip recovery | [K] |
| Record and review (one 30-60 second take) | Self-assessment | Mixed evidence, see 2.3 |
| Peer round (5-7 min) | Motivation, observational learning | Section 4 |
| Cool-down and log (5 min) | Close on something known and enjoyable (Suzuki ends sessions with review [S]); record one win and one next step | Evaluation phase [S~]; [K] |
| Handoff to parent (3 min) | Parent hears one thing, gets one home action | Suzuki "one point at a time" [S] |

### 2.2 Mentor role versus lead teacher

**Lead teacher (class):** introduces new material, sets the one or two goals for the week, writes them on the goal card, decides when a student advances, gives the parent the weekly assignment. Teaches 8-12 students.

**Practice mentor (supervised hour):** does not introduce new material or change the teacher's plan. Does the following **[K]**:

1. Greets, checks the goal card, helps set up the instrument.
2. Cues the routine: warm-up, block timer, break.
3. Check-in every 12 minutes: listen to 30-60 seconds, say one specific thing that was good (process and task focused, not generic praise), ask one question ("what will you try next?"), give one correction at most. Feedback research separates task, process and self-regulation feedback from praise about the person; person-level praise is least effective [S~, Hattie and Timperley; the often-quoted effect sizes 0.65 vs 0.15 I could not verify (unverified)]. Suzuki handbook, same idea: "calls attention to only one point at a time," because too many instructions at once discourage children [S].
4. Notes anything that needs the teacher (pain, wrong habit, instrument problem) on the goal card or an index slip.
5. Helps with the record-and-review step and the log.
6. Escalates immediately: injury or pain, distress, any safeguarding concern, and any hearing risk (too loud, ringing) to the lead.

**Who can be a mentor** **[K]**, with the El Sistema and Harmony Project evidence:

- **Older students and graduates.** Peer-to-peer instruction is "omnipresent as a practice" in El Sistema-inspired programs [S, Booth]. Harmony Project lists 70 peer mentors alongside 120 professional instructors [S, harmony-project.org]. Cross-age peer tutoring works best when used to review and consolidate learning rather than introduce new material, benefits from tutor training, and shows about +5 to +6 months in the EEF toolkit with mixed trial results [S~]. Practice review is exactly the consolidation use.
- **Volunteers** (church members, music teachers, college students). Same role, same training.
- **Safeguarding:** under-18 mentors are helpers and never count toward the two-adult minimum; policies commonly require two screened, unrelated adults per activity and treat related adults as one [S~, Brotherhood Mutual and Church Law & Tax summaries]. A mentor 18 or older still passes screening. Treat the host church or school's policy as the floor.

### 2.3 Recording practice

Self-recording and playback is widely recommended but the evidence for children is thin: a 2023 study of 50 children found that prompting self-assessment (via a robot) improved motivation and performance; a 2025 study of 25 advanced pianists found a small, non-significant effect of self-listening; self-evaluation accuracy appears to grow with maturity [S~]. So: make recording optional, short (30-60 seconds), with a fixed three-question review ("What did I want? What did I hear? What will I change?") led by a mentor for under-13s. Keep recordings on the program's device or the student's own device, deleted at the end of the session unless the family chooses to keep one. Consent for any recording is a family decision (see Open questions).

### 2.4 Student-to-mentor ratios (judgment, labeled as such)

No source I found gives instrument-specific ratios for supervised music practice. Reference points:

- Childcare guidance for school-age care: about 1:10 for ages 6-8 and 1:12 for ages 9-12 in centers; groups up to 30 [S~, childcare.gov summary]. That is supervision of children, not instructional feedback.
- Church child-protection policies: at least 2 adults for 1-10 children, 3 for 30 or more [S~, one church's policy].
- Academic tutoring meta-analysis looked at 1:1, 1:2 and 1:3+ as categories; larger effects when tutoring happens at least three days a week; I did not retrieve the per-ratio effect sizes [S~, Nickow, Oreopoulos and Quan].
- Digital piano labs are sold as 1 teacher console to 8 student stations, expandable [S~, vendor pages].

**Recommended ratios [K] (unverified):**

| Situation | Students per mentor |
|---|---|
| Default mixed-instrument practice | 6 |
| First 8 weeks, strings, winds or brass, ages 6-10 | 4 |
| Headphone keyboards or electric guitars, students 11+ | 8 |
| Pads and electronic drums | 6 |
| Graduate drop-in practice | 10-12 |
| Always | 2 screened adults in the building area; one lead teacher floating per 24 students |

At 1:6, a 12-minute check-in round gives every student 2 minutes (arithmetic); at 1:4, 3 minutes.

### 2.5 Mentor training and checklist

Training **[K]**: one 2-hour initial session and a 10-minute briefing each week; cross-age tutor training is explicitly named as essential in the EEF summary [S~].

Initial session topics: safeguarding and the two-adult rule; the check-in script; one-point feedback; hearing and posture safety; how to read a goal card; what to escalate; positive tone ("a friend and helper," avoid negative criticism, per Suzuki handbook [S]); confidentiality.

Mentor checklist (one page, laminated):

- Before: read this week's goal cards; set out timers, water, stickers; check the room's sound level plan.
- During: round-robin every 12 minutes; one praise, one question, one correction at most; watch posture and volume; watch the clock for breaks.
- After: collect log cards; enter the three-line weekly note; flag items for the teacher; reset room.

## 3. Space and equipment for simultaneous practice

### 3.1 Sound: the physics and the hearing limits

- **Exposure limits.** NIOSH recommends 85 dBA over an 8-hour shift with a 3 dB exchange rate [S, CDC]. A university guide gives time at level: 85 dB about 8 hours, 88 dB 4 hours, 91 dB 2 hours, 94 dB 1 hour, 100 dB 15 minutes, 110 dB 1-2 minutes [S, East Texas A&M page]. NIOSH adds that at an average of 94 dB(A) musicians can be at risk after about an hour [S].
- **Measured music levels.** NIOSH-cited college concert and symphonic band averages were 89-90 dB(A) with about 52% of musicians exceeding the daily dose limit; marching band snare drums 95-113, cymbals 106-118, bass drums 95-122 [S]. A university practice-room study found mean levels of 87-95 dB(A) with brass highest and 48% exceeding the allowable daily dose [S~].
- **Children's headphones.** The WHO-ITU safe listening standard defines an adult mode of 80 dB for 40 hours a week and a child mode of 75 dB for 40 hours a week [S, WHO page].
- **Adding sources.** N equal sources raise the level above one source by 10 x log10(N) dB: 6 players +7.8 dB, 12 players +10.8 dB, 24 players +13.8 dB (arithmetic **[K]**; assumes incoherent equal sources in a diffuse field, which a reverberant hall approximates). Example: if each beginner averages 70 dBA near the instrument, 12 acoustic players in one hall are around 80 dBA in the room, and 24 around 84 (illustrative, **unverified**).
- **Isolation.** A conference paper summarizing ISO 23591 says Sound Transmission Class (STC) 60 is expected for music performance spaces [S~]. A hosted church hall cannot meet that, which is why separation by distance, rooms and scheduling matters more than treatment.
- **Practice pads and electronic kits.** Practice pads and e-kits are far quieter than acoustic drums, but published decibel numbers come from low-reliability sources (one guide: 48-56 dB for mesh e-kits vs 108-112 dB acoustic; another: 60-75 vs 120+) and kick-pedal or floor-borne thump often matters more than the head [S~]. Plan to measure rather than trust a table.

### 3.2 Recommended equipment per student (ideal) **[K]**

| Instrument | Practice equipment | Sound control |
|---|---|---|
| Keyboard, piano | Digital piano or weighted keyboard, headphones, bench or chair, footstool for small children | Headphones; optional teacher console (lab controllers support 1 teacher to 8 student stations, or 2 students per keyboard in split mode [S~]) |
| Guitar, bass | Acoustic guitar for beginners (quiet) or electric with a headphone amp; footstool | Headphone amp for electric; acoustic guitar at modest volume |
| Drums, percussion | Practice pad with stand per student; mesh-head electronic kit for one or two students at a time | Pads and mesh heads; floor isolation pad under any pedal |
| Violin, viola, cello | Correctly sized instrument, music stand, chair with right height, mute for early weeks | Practice mutes; separate room when more than 4 players |
| Winds and brass | Instrument, stand, chair; mutes for brass | Separate room; hearing protection if levels run high; short blocks |
| All | Metronome or tuner, pencil, goal card, practice log | |

Each student gets personal headphones or washable ear pads; no sharing without cleaning **[K]**.

### 3.3 Floor area and layout **[K] unless cited**

- **Reference points.** Planning guide rule of thumb: about 30 square feet per instrumental musician [S~]. A 1983 military band design guide lists small individual practice rooms at about 65 net square feet minimum with at least 8 feet in the shortest dimension, large group practice rooms about 700 NSF, ceilings 10-12 feet preferred, 8-9 feet acceptable for individual rooms; room dimensions should not be equal or integral multiples to limit standing waves [S~]. A commercial design guide calls 10 x 12 feet a good solo practice room and warns against a 10 x 10 square [S~, informal]. A Wenger document gives 2,500 square feet for a 60-75 student band or orchestra rehearsal room with 18-22 foot ceilings [S~].
- **Per student in a shared practice hall (recommended):** about 25 square feet for a headphone keyboard station (instrument, bench, a clear 3 feet behind); about 35-40 square feet for acoustic instruments with spacing and music stands. For 12 students, plan 300-450 square feet; for 24, 600-900 square feet (arithmetic from the per-student numbers).
- **Open hall vs rooms.** Ideal: a main hall for quiet-by-design instruments (keyboards and electric guitars on headphones, pads) and two or three small rooms (65-120 square feet) for strings, winds and brass. A single open hall works only with headphone-first instruments or with acoustic instruments in small groups spread in separate corners, with absorbent surfaces (rugs, curtains, portable absorptive screens). Portable screens reduce reverberation and visual distraction, not isolation **[K]**.
- **Sight lines.** For safeguarding and supervision, every practice room needs a window or open door and the mentor must be able to see every student from the check-in route **[K]**.
- **Avoid sanctuaries for simultaneous acoustic practice:** reverberant spaces raise the effective level of every player **[K]**.

### 3.4 Hearing-safety rules to post in the room **[K]**

1. Target room level 80 dBA or below for the shared hall; measure with a calibrated meter if available; a phone meter app is only a rough check **[K]**.
2. Headphone volume at or below a comfortable "can hear a mentor speak" level; use volume-limited headphones for children (check the manufacturer's maximum; WHO child mode is 75 dB for 40 hours a week [S]).
3. Brass, drums and loud voices in the separate room; hearing protection offered when levels run high.
4. Quiet breaks are part of the schedule (NIOSH advice to musicians includes quiet breaks [S]).
5. Tell families to report ringing, muffled hearing or ear pain the same day [S, NIOSH warning signs: tinnitus, distortion, diplacusis, hyperacusis].

## 4. Using the hour for peer learning and for parents

### 4.1 Peer learning

- **Why it works.** Suzuki programs treat group lessons and observing other students as aids to motivation: "children love to do what they see other children do," and they may learn from advanced students and peers "possibly more than they do from adult teacher directly" [S, handbook]. El Sistema programs make group, ensemble and sectional learning the primary mode and have students teach each other [S, Booth]. A Norwegian horn-studio report describes students performing for each other under set rules and choosing the kind of feedback they want; weaker students benefited from being grouped with stronger ones [S~]. Observing a masterclass can itself teach, when observation is guided [S~].
- **Cautions.** One practitioner who studied peer teaching in Venezuela admitted romanticizing it after seeing an older student teach an uninterested beginner [S~]. Peer tutoring trials in England showed null results for one cross-age model and a revised headline of about +5 months [S~]. So: structure it, train it, and limit it to review.

**Peer formats inside the hour (all [K]):**

1. **Pair practice (5-7 min):** two students of the same level play the same passage alternately, "listen and say one thing you liked and one thing to try."
2. **Play-along (5 min):** everyone plays the week's piece together at slow tempo, or plays with a recording (Suzuki listening is the base of that method [S]).
3. **Mini-performance (5 min):** one or two students perform for the room; applause, one positive comment from a peer; the same students do not always go first.
4. **Buddy mentor:** a graduate or older student sits with a group of four for one block (always with a screened adult in view).
5. **Monthly mini-concert:** 15 minutes at the end of the practice hour, parents invited; the program of the day is the pieces students are practicing.

### 4.2 For parents (the waiting family member)

- **Suzuki parent role** [S, handbook]: attend lessons, learn alongside the child, act as "home teacher," call attention to one point at a time, stay positive and present "as a friend and helper." Parents need not have music background. Attendance is typically phased out around age 13 in Suzuki programs [S~, summaries].
- **El Sistema family model:** parents attend classes with the youngest students, families learn how to support learning, and some sites run parent ensembles [S, Booth].
- **A community music school stance:** parents are "instrumental in creating and monitoring a culture of practice at home," and young students cannot manage their own practice until well into high school, so they need help setting manageable goals [S, UNCSA].
- **Parent involvement research:** most successful young musicians have highly involved parents who follow rather than lead [S~, Davidson et al. 1996, from secondary reports; I did not read the paper].

**What to offer in the hour [K]:**

- A **parent corner** within sight of the practice area, with a seat, a quiet corner for older siblings, and a one-page "how to help" sheet.
- A **10-minute "how to support practice at home" mini-session**, once a week on a rotating topic: setting a daily 10-minute time; one point at a time; praise effort; listening at home; what to do when a child resists; small sound-safe practice at home.
- **Opt-in observation:** parents may watch a block through the window or door, but do not coach during the supervised hour unless invited.
- **Three-minute handoff:** the student shows one thing; the mentor gives one sentence about what went well and one home action; the log card goes home.
- A **parent note** (section 5): one line a week, by email.

## 5. Tracking (low-tech first, no accounts)

Gamo Academy has no accounts (AGENTS.md, 2026-11-16). Tracking therefore uses paper, forms and email.

### 5.1 The practice log

- **Paper goal-and-log card** (one card per student per month, kept in the student's folder and carried home): this week's one or two goals from the teacher; each day's practice (check boxes); one win; one next step. Rationale: planning, monitoring and evaluating one's own practice are the core of self-regulated learning [S~, McPherson and Zimmerman]; the EEF toolkit reports metacognition and self-regulation as about +7 months over a year (primary +8, secondary +7) at very low cost, strongest when taught explicitly in the subject and on challenging tasks, with the evidence security rated high but weakened by developer-run studies [S~].
- **What to log:** day, minutes (round to 5), goal practiced, 3-face smile scale, one sentence. Avoid long forms; compliance drops fast **[K]**.
- **Do not use the log as a performance ranking.** Practice time alone is not quality: in the Hallam work, students who failed exams used less effective strategies and enjoyed lessons less, so tracking should reward process and effort, not minutes [S~]; Suzuki warns against pushing beyond the teacher's assignment [S].

### 5.2 Goals and badges

- **Goals:** two per week, specific and observable ("play bars 5-8 at 60 beats per minute three times in a row without stopping").
- **Badges (non-competitive):** earned for process, such as "practiced 4 days," "tried a new strategy," "helped a partner." Suzuki uses practice cards and stickers as rewards [S]. No ranking, no prizes tied to payment or sponsorship (docs/GAMO_NONPROFIT_MODEL.md governance rules).
- **Level checkpoints:** short, friendly "show a teacher" moments rather than exams **[K]**.

### 5.3 Simple dashboard

A weekly one-page staff view in the admin app (already the only dashboard): attendance, goal met yes/no/partly, a mentor flag. No student-facing dashboard. Data minimal; no personal data in logs, per the project rules.

### 5.4 Weekly progress note to parents (email)

Sent by staff from the admin app, after each session; the subject never contains the student's name (AGENTS.md). Template **[K]**:

> This week we practiced [goal]. One win: [specific thing]. One thing to try at home: [10-minute task]. Next class: [one line]. Reply to this email with questions.

Three lines, plain text, sent the same evening. Feedback research says feedback is most useful when it states where the learner is going, how they are going and where to next [S~, Hattie and Timperley], and weaker when it is praise about the person [S~]. The note follows the same three questions. Mentors enter their check-in notes in a short staff form (3 fields: goal met, one win, one concern); the lead teacher edits and sends. Parents manage preferences and unsubscribe by the signed link pattern already used for Academy manage links (AGENTS.md).

## 6. Variants and how supervised practice evolves

### 6.1 Same day vs separate days

| Variant | Strengths | Weaknesses | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Class then practice, same day (recommended default)** | Immediate feedback; one trip for families; best for transport-limited families; parent can attend both | Long day for young children; sleep gap before next touch | Indirect [S, S~] |
| **Class one day, supervised practice 2-3 days later (e.g., Saturday and Wednesday evening)** | Spaced repetition; shorter days | Two trips; weaker fit when a family member must wait | Spacing helps motor learning on average (reported d = 0.79 in one meta-analysis, <10% of studies motor; **unverified**), but music-specific spacing data are inconclusive [S~] |
| **Hybrid: same-day practice plus a 30-minute mid-week open practice** | Best of both; second touch within 3-4 days | Needs a second slot and staff | [K] |

My recommendation: same-day for everyone in the first two terms; add the optional mid-week open practice for students who miss home practice.

### 6.2 Drop-in practice for graduates

- A weekly open practice of 60 minutes with 1 mentor per 10-12 students **[K]**.
- Sign-in on paper; students choose their own goal and write it on the card.
- Graduates earn a path to **mentor-in-training**: after 3 terms in the program, with the lead teacher's recommendation. Compare Harmony Project's 70 peer mentors [S] and El Sistema's habit of early peer teaching [S].
- The research caution about peer teaching applies: train and structure it [S~].

### 6.3 How supervision fades as students become independent **[K]**

| Stage | Weeks | Mentor ratio | Who sets goals | Parent role |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Fully guided | 1-8 | 1:4 | Teacher | Present, watching, learning |
| 2. Guided with choices | 9-24 | 1:6 | Teacher sets one, student sets one | Waiting in corner; weekly mini-session |
| 3. Student-led | 25+ | 1:8 | Student with teacher approval | Handoff note only |

## 8. Trade-offs when constrained

Short, because the ideal above is the target.

- **No separate rooms.** Use the sound-tier rule: all headphone keyboards, pads and quiet acoustic guitars in one hall; loud acoustic instruments, and brass move to a rotating 20-minute slot in a separate corner or room. Reduce active playing blocks to 8-10 minutes for acoustic instruments in the shared space.
- **No digital pianos.** Use paper keyboards and quiet rhythm and listening work; or restrict keyboard sessions to one student per instrument in turn, which cuts capacity.
- **Fewer mentors.** Raise ratio to 1:8 only for headphone and pad stations; reduce active playing for young students; extend peer pairing.
- **Shorter hour.** Keep the structure (goal, two blocks, log, handoff) and cut the peer round to 3 minutes, total 45 minutes; do not cut the handoff.
- **No screened adults.** Do not run the supervised hour without them; the program should refuse to open a session rather than relax this.
- **Hearing.** If room levels cannot be controlled, shorten blocks, move to headphone-first, use hearing protection for loud instruments.
- **Transport.** If families cannot make a second trip, same-day practice is the right default.

## 9. Recommended session blueprint (one page)

**Audience:** ages 6-17 and adults; cohorts of 12, grouped by age band and instrument family. **Site:** one classroom, one practice hall of quiet-by-design instruments, 1-3 small rooms for loud instruments, a parent corner in sight.

**Weekly shape:** Class 45 minutes (30 for ages 6-8, 60 for 13+). Break 10 minutes. Supervised practice 60 minutes. Total about 2 hours with transitions. Home: 10 minutes the next day plus 3-4 more short days (Suzuki: short and often [S]).

**Staff:** 1 lead teacher per cohort of 12; mentors at 1:6 (1:4 for first 8 weeks of strings, winds, brass, ages 6-10); 2 screened unrelated adults in the area; under-18 mentors are helpers only.

**Supervised hour (60 minutes):**

| Min | What happens | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 0-5 | Arrival, instrument set-up, tuning, find the goal card | Mentor greets, checks card |
| 5-10 | Warm-up: body, then known material | Open with something known [S, Suzuki] |
| 10-25 | Block 1: today's first goal (age 6-8: 8-10 min; 13+: 15 min) | Check-in at minute 12: listen 30-60 sec, one praise, one question, one correction at most |
| 25-30 | Break: water, stretch, quiet listening; lip rest | Hearing break |
| 30-45 | Block 2: second goal, or the piece; one 30-60 second recording and review (optional) | Three-question review |
| 45-52 | Peer round: pairs, play-along or mini-performance | Review and consolidation, not new material [S~] |
| 52-57 | Cool-down: replay the one most important passage, then something fun; fill in the log (one win, one next step) | Reactivation before stopping [S~] |
| 57-60 | Handoff: student shows the parent one thing; mentor says one win and one home action | Parent hears one point only [S, Suzuki] |

**Rules posted in the room:** one point at a time; praise the process; room level at or below 80 dBA, headphone volume low enough to hear a mentor speak, loud instruments in the separate room; report pain, ringing or distress to the lead.

**After the hour:** mentor completes a 3-field staff form; the lead sends a three-line email the same evening (no student name in the subject).

**Progress:** goal-and-log card each month; process badges; a monthly mini-concert at the end of the practice hour.

## 10. Implications for Gamo Academy (concrete numbers)

1. **Adopt the 60-minute supervised practice hour for all ages, with active playing of 20-25 min (6-8), 30-35 (9-12), 40-45 (13-17), 45-50 (adults).** Reason: attention limits and typical beginner practice (23 min a day at Preliminary level [S]).
2. **Staff at 1 mentor per 6 students** (1:4 early beginners on strings, winds, brass; 1:8 for headphone stations), **plus one lead teacher per 12-student class**. For 24 students: 1 teacher, 4 mentors, 1 coordinator on one classroom and one hall in staggered waves; for 48: 2 teachers, 8 mentors, 1 coordinator, 2 classrooms.
3. **Put the mentor on a 12-minute check-in round** with 2-3 minutes per student.
4. **Plan equipment first:** one digital piano or weighted keyboard and one pair of headphones per keyboard student; practice pads or mesh electronic kit for drums; headphone amps for electric guitars; practice mutes for strings and brass. Do not put loud acoustic drum kits in the shared hour **[K]**.
5. **Space:** about 25 sq ft per headphone station and 35-40 sq ft per acoustic player; 300-450 sq ft for 12, 600-900 sq ft for 24; 1-3 small rooms of 65-120 sq ft for strings, winds, brass. Never run simultaneous acoustic practice in a reverberant sanctuary.
6. **Hearing:** room target 80 dBA or below; NIOSH reference 85 dBA for 8 hours, 94 dBA for 1 hour [S]; WHO child headphone mode 75 dB for 40 hours a week [S]; offer protection for loud instruments; build a quiet break into every hour.
7. **Parents:** one parent corner, one weekly 10-minute coaching mini-session, one 3-minute handoff, one 3-line weekly email.
8. **Tracking with no accounts:** paper goal-and-log card, a 3-field staff form, the existing admin dashboard for attendance and flags, and the existing signed-link email pattern for preferences. No ranking and no practice-time leaderboard.
9. **Mentor pipeline:** 2-hour initial training, 10-minute weekly briefing, screened adults for safeguarding, under-18 helpers only as helpers, graduates become mentors-in-training after about 3 terms.
10. **Start same-day**, add optional mid-week open practice, and plan drop-in practice for graduates at 1:10-12.
11. **Mission guardrails (AGENTS.md):** no fees or tiers are proposed here; mentor stipends, equipment costs and any parent contributions are owner decisions; practice-hour language stays educational and mission-based.

## 11. Open questions for the owner

1. **Instruments offered first.** Which instruments (keyboard, guitar, strings, winds, drums) will Gamo Academy offer at launch? The room and equipment plan depends on it.
2. **Hosts.** Which host types (church hall, school classroom, partner venue) must the plan support? Church sanctuaries are a poor practice space; classrooms and fellowship halls are better.
3. **Age bands.** Is the first cohort children only (6-12), teens, adults, or all? Adults change the schedule (late evenings, fewer parents).
4. **Mentor model.** Volunteers only, older students, paid assistants, or a mix? Any payment for mentors or instructors is an owner and board decision and must stay separate from any Gamo administrative allocation (project rules).
5. **Screening.** What screening level and policy will Gamo require for mentors (background checks, references, training)? Will it follow the host church or school's rule if it is stricter?
6. **Equipment and instruments.** Who supplies instruments and headphones (program, family, host)? Any equipment funding or loan model is an owner approval.
7. **Recording and photography.** What are the consent rules for recording a student's practice, and should recordings ever leave the room?
8. **Practice hour length.** Is a fixed 60 minutes required for every age, or may under-9 cohorts use 45 minutes?
9. **Day pattern.** Saturday morning block, two weekday evenings, or both? This changes late-evening and school-night limits.
10. **Parent presence.** Must a family member stay on site for under-13s (safeguarding and Suzuki model), and may older teens wait elsewhere?
11. **Hearing measures.** Is the program willing to buy a basic sound-level meter and volume-limited headphones as a first equipment line?
