# Gamo Academy: age bands and curriculum design (ages 4-17)

Research brief 02. Scope: how classes should be designed for ages 4-5 (if at all), 6-8, 9-12 and 13-17. Adult-specific design is covered elsewhere. Date: 2026-11-16. Owner direction: describe the ideal setup first with no compromise; constraints appear only in the short "Trade-offs when constrained" section near the end.

Gamo Academy model assumed throughout: classes at church, school or partner venues; the student arrives with a family member who drives; a class of 30-60 minutes is followed by a supervised practice session of about an hour. Instruments: piano/keyboard (6+), acoustic and electric guitar (7+), bass (9+), drums and percussion (7+), voice (8+, worship vocals and general).

## Evidence labels

- **[S]** read in a source this session (the page or PDF was opened and the statement checked against it).
- **[S*]** seen only as a search-result summary this session; the page itself was not opened or could not be opened (several vendor and exam-board sites returned HTTP 403). Treat as lower confidence and verify before it appears on a public page.
- **[K]** prior knowledge or my judgment, unverified. Numbers I could not source are marked "(unverified)".

Important honesty note: there is no single authoritative standard for "ideal class length by age" or "practice minutes by age". The figures below are a synthesis of method guides (Suzuki, Yamaha), child-development milestones (CDC, MedlinePlus), research on motivation and injury, and teacher practice. Where sources are teacher blogs, I say so.

## Summary

- **Start instrument lessons at the owner's minimums, and treat ages 4-5 as an optional parent-and-child "Foundations" class, not instrument lessons.** CDC lists "pays attention for 5 to 10 minutes" at age 5 [S]; MedlinePlus says a 6-year-old can focus on a task for about 15 minutes and a 9-year-old for about an hour [S]. Suzuki programs start at 3-4 [S] and Yamaha's Junior Music Course at 4-5 [S*], but the largest twin-and-musician study found little support for a causal advantage of starting before age 8 once total practice is accounted for [S]. There is no evidence-based reason to rush.
- **Design every class as short segments, 6-12 minutes each, with a visible rhythm.** Ages 6-8: 45-minute class in groups of 4-6; ages 9-12: 45-60 minutes in groups of 4-8; ages 13-17: 60-minute class that ends in an ensemble. The supervised practice hour is structured too: two focused instrument blocks with a break, never one unbroken hour for under-10s.
- **Parent role changes by band.** Ages 6-8: parent in the room as "practice partner" (the Suzuki "home teacher" model, which is most developed for young children [S]). Ages 9-12: parent in the last 5-10 minutes and on site. Ages 13-17: parent on site or at drop-off, student owns the practice plan. Self-determination research says autonomy, competence and relatedness drive whether teens continue [S].
- **Mix ear and reading by band.** Ear/imitation first for 6-8 (Suzuki delayed reading [S]; songs-first modern band [S]), balanced by 9-12, chart and notation fluency plus improvisation for 13-17. Use worship songs, hymns and general repertoire in all bands.
- **Size the instrument to the body, not the age.** Guitar: 1/4 to 3/4 by height and a fit test, nylon strings first [S]. Piano: adjustable bench, footstool, small-hand repertoire; hand span at age 6 can be as small as 5.8 inches [S]. Drums: electronic or practice-pad first, throne set so thighs slope slightly downward [S*].
- **Hearing, posture and safeguarding are design requirements, not afterthoughts.** A 2019 review proposed an 8-hour limit of 80 dBA for children's recreational noise [S]. Playing-related pain is common even in young players (71% at ages 9-13 in a contest-level German sample) [S], and the main prevention measures are warm-ups, breaks, posture and open communication [S]. For child safety, the CDC youth-serving-organization guide recommends screening, at least two adults present, visible spaces and windows in doors [S].
- **Use an in-house Level 0-4 ladder with short teacher-led checkpoints, and treat graded exams (ABRSM, RCM, Trinity Rock & Pop) as optional outside benchmarks.** None of them has a minimum age or requires earlier grades at the entry level [S][S*]. Studio practices such as over-emphasis on exams and unexplained scale drilling are named in the motivation literature as risks to motivation [S].
- **Typical time:** about 3-3.5 years of weekly attendance to reach Level 4 (unverified; an external teacher source says a steady student starting at 8 might reach ABRSM Grade 1 around age 11 [S*]).

## 1. Developmental baseline

| Age | What the sources say | Design consequence |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | CDC (milestones most children, 75%+, reach by this age): pays attention for 5 to 10 minutes during activities like story time; counts to 10; writes some letters of their name; follows rules and takes turns in games; sings, dances or acts for others [S] | Segments of 5-8 minutes; singing and movement are natural; turn-taking games work; no reading of staff notation expected |
| 6 | MedlinePlus: a 6-year-old should focus on a task for about 15 minutes; can follow 3 commands in a row; fine motor skills vary widely; peer acceptance grows in importance [S] | Segments of 8-12 minutes; give one instruction at a time, three at most |
| 9 | MedlinePlus: by 9 a child should focus for about an hour; by 10 can follow 5 commands; reading shifts from decoding to understanding content by third grade [S] | Class up to 45-60 minutes is feasible; multi-step assignments possible; written chord charts and notation become practical |
| 11-15 | Voice change research on boys aged 11.5-15.9 found later-stage changing voices were judged more agile than earlier-stage ones [S*] | Check vocal range every few weeks for boys and some girls; adjust parts and keys [K for girls] |
| 13-17 | Self-determination study: peer relatedness had comparatively high effect sizes in adolescence; music is closely tied to adolescents' social identity [S] | Cohorts by age; peer ensembles; choice of repertoire |

Caveat: MedlinePlus attention figures are general health-education numbers, not music-specific, and they are higher than some teacher estimates (a teacher-blog source gives 12-18 minutes at ages 5-6 and 16-24 at 7-8 [S*], unverified). I therefore plan for the lower end: a segment lasts 8-12 minutes at 6-8, and a long class is made of many segments.

Orff (imitate, explore, improvise, compose [S*]) and Kodály (singing as the base, sequence set by child development [S*]) both argue for play-based, singing-first early instruction. Neither source gave age-specific minute counts in what I could retrieve.

## 2. Age bands

### 2.1 Ages 4-5 (optional "Foundations", not instrument lessons)

**Recommendation:** do not offer piano, guitar, bass, drums or voice lessons below the owner's minimum ages. If the owner wants a 4-5 entry point, offer a 30-minute parent-and-child group class (singing, movement, rhythm, simple percussion, listening) and call it Foundations. Offer it only after the core 6+ program is stable. This is an owner decision (see Open questions).

Why:
- Attention: 5-10 minutes of sustained attention at age 5 [S]; a 4-year-old is lower [K].
- The early-start advantage is largely explained by family and practice factors, not training before age 8 [S] (Wesseldijk et al. as summarized by the British Psychological Society; the original paper was not opened).
- Established programs that start here are group, parent-attended and ear-first: Suzuki lessons may begin at 3-4 [S]; Yamaha Junior Music Course starts at ages 4-5, two years, one class a week, parent participation required, groups of about 6-10 in one regional listing [S*].
- A small controlled study (45 children aged 5-7) found five-year-olds in group piano outperformed private peers, while six- and seven-year-olds in private lessons outperformed group peers on kinesthetic response; overall difference was null; the author offers growing self-consciousness and sociability as explanations [S]. Suggestive only (small n, single thesis).

**Foundations class, 30 minutes, 6-10 children each with a caregiver, once a week (no minute counts longer than 6):**

| Minutes | Segment |
|---|---|
| 0-3 | Hello song, name song, caregiver and child sit together |
| 3-8 | Steady-beat movement with a rhythm stick or egg shaker; echo patterns |
| 8-14 | Singing: two or three short songs, matching pitch in a comfortable range, a hymn or children's worship song learned by rote |
| 14-20 | Instrument exploration: tuned percussion, drum, keyboard "high and low" game, no formal technique |
| 20-26 | Story or game with music (freeze dance, listening to a short recorded piece) |
| 26-30 | Goodbye song and one 2-minute home listening or movement task |

Parent involvement: in the room, participating. Home: daily 5-minute listening or singing, not practice [K]. Frequency: once a week. Assessment: none; a "Level 0 readiness" observation (can keep the beat for 8 bars, echo a 3-note pattern, follow two instructions) [K]. Recital: informal sharing at the end of a term, 10 minutes, parents only [K].

### 2.2 Ages 6-8

**Starting ages (ideal):** piano 6 (a hand position of five fingers is fine, but expect small spans: the youngest child in one pianist hand-span database, a 6-year-old girl, had a thumb-to-little-finger span of 5.8 inches [S]); guitar 7 on a 1/4 or 1/2 nylon guitar [S for sizes]; drums 7 on pads, then a junior kit [K]; voice starts at 8 (so for 6-7, only class singing in Foundations or piano classes) [K]; bass starts at 9 per owner (6-8s can do general keyboard or percussion first).

**Ideal class length and shape:** 45 minutes, group of 4-6 same-instrument children (or, for piano and keyboard, a lab with headphones), plus a teaching assistant, a second adult in the room at all times. Short individual contact is built in because the one controlled study suggests 6-7-year-olds may do better with individual attention than younger children [S].

| Minutes | Segment | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 0-5 | Arrival ritual and body warm-up | Same routine every week: posture, hand warm-up (finger "wake-up"), breathing; parent beside child |
| 5-12 | Review: "play for me" | Each child plays last week's piece for the group or teacher, 1-2 minutes each, others tap the beat |
| 12-22 | Technique and one new concept | Two 5-minute chunks (for example hand shape plus rhythm reading with rhythm syllables) |
| 22-32 | New repertoire by ear/imitation | Teacher models a phrase, children echo, then repeat; add letter names or notation only as support |
| 32-38 | Play-along or small ensemble | Backing track or teacher accompaniment; children play the same pattern together |
| 38-43 | Game | Beat-keeping game, note-name race, "name that rhythm"; competition is cooperative (the whole group earns a point) [K] |
| 43-45 | Assignment and "practice card" | One or two things only; parent initials the practice card at home (Suzuki programs use practice cards and stickers, and counting objects for repetitions [S]) |

**Weekly rhythm:** one visit: 45-minute class, a 10-minute break and snack, then supervised practice. For 6-8 I recommend the practice session be at most 45-60 minutes made of two 15-minute instrument blocks with a partner or parent, a movement break, and a 10-15-minute listening or ear-training game (see section 5). Home practice: 5 short days, 10-15 minutes, ideally two chunks of 5-8 minutes.

**Parent involvement:** in the room for class (observing and learning the assignment), practice partner at home and in the supervised session. Suzuki guidance: parents attend lessons, supervise listening and practice with the child each day [S]; the NIU program expects a parent to attend all lessons [S]; the Music Institute of Chicago handbook says parents need not be musicians to be good practice supervisors (SAA-affiliated sources say similar [S*]). Dr. Suzuki advised that practice periods grow with concentration: "two or three minutes ... for a beginner ... four or five times a day" [S, quoted from Nurtured by Love in the Music Institute of Chicago handbook]. A practice session is easier when the parent calls attention to only one point at a time [S].

**Instrument sizing:** see section 3. Key: guitar 1/4 (about ages 4-6, about 3'9") to 1/2 (about ages 5-8, about 4'3") [S]; a tall child may need a bigger size, so use height and a fit test [S]. Piano: footstool so feet are supported, bench height so forearm is about level with the keys [S*]. Drums: junior kit or pads, throne set so feet reach pedals, cymbals no higher than shoulder [S*].

**Reading vs ear:** about 70% ear and imitation, 30% reading readiness (rhythm syllables, steps and skips, finding notes on a five-line staff in one position) [K]. Suzuki delays reading until playing is established, "just as we teach children to read a language only after they can speak" [S]; NIU says many Suzuki children begin to read notes within the first year [S].

**Repertoire:** 30% worship songs and hymns in singable, small-hand arrangements (three chords or five-finger melodies, for example public-domain "Jesus Loves Me", "Joyful, Joyful, We Adore Thee") [K]; 50% general (folk songs, nursery tunes, simple classical themes, movie-style melodies); 20% child's choice from a teacher-approved list. All copyrighted worship songs need licensing clarity (Open questions).

**Motivation techniques:** practice cards and stickers (informational, not a prize) [S]; group lessons and performances are motivating because children aspire to older students [S]; short playable songs in week 1; "play for the group" every class; family listening; a visible Level 0-1 map. Avoid heavy contingent rewards, which the self-determination study says can undermine motivation [S].

**Assessment and milestones:** in-house Level 0-1 checkpoints (5 minutes, teacher-assessed, pass/"not yet", no marks). ABRSM's Prep Test is optional, 10 minutes, no marks, aimed at learners about six to nine months in (the last is from tutor sites [S*]); ABRSM allows entry at any age [S*]. RCM has Preparatory A/B (piano) and Introductory/Preparatory levels for non-piano before Level 1 [S].

**Recital cadence:** a 15-minute class sharing every 6 weeks; a short term-end recital with 1-2 minutes per child, with parents; no competitions. NIU Suzuki students participate in at least one recital per semester [S].

**Safety:** hearing protection (drums); frequent breaks at least every 10-15 minutes [K]; no fixed-height furniture; two screened adults always (see section 3.6).

### 2.3 Ages 9-12

**Starting ages:** bass 9+ (short-scale; see section 3); electric guitar from 9 is fine (thin, low-tension strings are easy to press, though heavier [S]); voice 8+ continues; all other instruments also take beginners here, with a compressed ladder (about 20-30% faster, unverified [K]).

**Ideal class length and shape:** 45 minutes (Level 0-2) to 60 minutes (Level 3+ and ensemble classes), group of 4-8. MedlinePlus: focus extends toward an hour by age 9 [S]; a teacher source suggests 45-minute lessons "seem ideal" for ages 9-15 [S*, unverified].

| Minutes | Segment (60-minute version) |
|---|---|
| 0-5 | Warm-up and posture check (hands, breath, quiet start) |
| 5-15 | Review and individual feedback: each plays last week's goal, peers give one "glow" (positive observation) and one suggestion |
| 15-30 | Technique and theory in context: scale or pattern, rhythm reading, chords |
| 30-45 | New repertoire: learn by ear first, then notation or chord chart |
| 45-55 | Play-along or small ensemble: group piece with parts (melody, chords, bass, rhythm) |
| 55-58 | Optional challenge game or composition, 3 minutes |
| 58-60 | Assignment: 2-3 goals written in a practice log |

(45-minute version: shorten technique to 8 minutes, repertoire to 12, ensemble to 8.)

**Weekly rhythm:** one visit; supervised practice about 60 minutes (two 20-25-minute instrument blocks with a break and an ear-training or composition mini-session). Home practice 4-5 days of 15-25 minutes.

**Parent involvement:** shift from in-room to on-site. Parent comes into the last 5-10 minutes of class, hears one piece, and reads the written assignment; parent is a "practice supporter" rather than daily co-practicer. Research on Suzuki-style parent roles is mostly about younger children, and in a studio study the most effective parents elicited their child's views on how involved they should be and negotiated practice with them [S*, Creech 2010, abstract level only]. This supports a gradual release.

**Reading vs ear:** 50/50: lead sheets with chord symbols, rhythm notation, tablature for guitar and bass, simple staff reading for piano and voice [K]. Reading grows in importance as school reading shifts to comprehension by third grade [S].

**Repertoire:** 40% worship songs and hymns (including chord-chart accompaniment of congregational songs), 40% general/popular and classical, 20% student choice. Introduce transposing and Nashville-number charts at Level 3 [K].

**Motivation and retention:** optimal challenge over "making it easy or fun": the self-determination study says teachers should spend time making lessons optimally challenging, creating interest and new skills (competence), providing rewarding peer relationships (relatedness) and input into the curriculum (autonomy) [S]. The same study warns that "bribing students with rewards", sub-optimal challenge and contingent rewards can subvert motivation [S]. Ensembles by age band so friendships form.

**Assessment and milestones:** Level 1-3 checkpoints. External benchmarks: RCM Levels 1-4 and ABRSM Grades 1-2 are plausible aims for the most committed students; Trinity Rock & Pop (guitar, bass, drums, keyboards, vocals; three songs plus a skills test in face-to-face; no age limits; no prerequisites [S]) fit the contemporary tracks. Typical external pace is 12-18 months per grade (tutor source [S*], unverified).

**Recital cadence:** monthly in-class sharing; two formal recitals per year (about every 16-18 weeks, [K]) plus optional participation in a church service or community event, always optional.

**Safety:** hearing (see 3.5), posture/RSI warm-ups, growth checks on instrument size every 6 months (guitar fit test repeated about every six months, [S]).

### 2.4 Ages 13-17

**Starting ages:** all instruments including bass and voice take beginners. Voice: girls and boys are in or near voice change; assess range regularly and avoid forcing extremes [S* for boys; K for girls]. Teens starting from zero should sit in a teen cohort with a faster ladder, not in a class of 7-year-olds (relatedness [S]).

**Ideal class length and shape:** 60 minutes, group 4-8, ending in an ensemble.

| Minutes | Segment |
|---|---|
| 0-5 | Warm-up and goals check-in: each student states one goal for the day (autonomy) |
| 5-15 | Review and peer feedback |
| 15-30 | Technique, theory and ear training integrated with repertoire (scales, chord construction, rhythm reading) |
| 30-45 | Repertoire: student-chosen song learned by ear and chart |
| 45-58 | Band or ensemble rehearsal: roles rotate (lead, rhythm, bass, drums, vocals) |
| 58-60 | Assignment and log |

**Weekly rhythm:** one visit (60-minute class + 60-minute supervised practice, the second hour used for ensemble rehearsal or self-directed practice). Optional second weekly ensemble or worship-team rehearsal. Home practice 4-5 days of 20-40 minutes. Practice should be capped by health guidance: in a contest-level German sample, students practicing over 21 hours/week had playing-related pain at 94% and mean practice was about 7 hours 18 minutes a week [S]; Gamo's intended load is far lower.

**Parent involvement:** on site or at drop-off; parent receives an email summary (Gamo uses email, not accounts) and attends recitals. The student owns the practice plan and log. In-room attendance only on request.

**Reading vs ear:** 40% ear and improvisation, 60% reading and charts, with a clear route to full notation or lead-sheet fluency for anyone wanting graded exams [K]. Modern band guidance: students choose personal goals, learn by ear, write and record songs, and the approach "is the entry point, not the destination" [S].

**Repertoire:** 40% student-chosen contemporary and worship, 30% hymns and congregational songs played in a band arrangement, 30% general or classical. Songwriting and arranging projects.

**Motivation and retention:** autonomy (choose songs, set goals), relatedness (ensemble, same-age peers, mentoring younger students), competence (clear next-level targets). Evans, McPherson and Davidson: decisions to stop were associated with diminished competence, relatedness and autonomy [S]. Evans and McPherson (2015): children with a long-term identity as a musician played better and kept playing longer; 157 children aged 7-9, followed for 10 years; participants in schools with a visible instrumental program were 2.6 times more likely to have a long-term identity [S]. After 5 years only 41% of the original sample were still in a school band program [S], which is the retention baseline to beat.

**Assessment and milestones:** Level 3-4 checkpoints; optional RCM, ABRSM or Trinity exams; teens can also do a "performance portfolio" (video of three songs) [K] mirroring Trinity's three-song format [S].

**Recital cadence:** two formal showcases per year, band-led; optional worship-service participation with parent consent.

**Safety:** hearing (amps, drums, in-ear), RSI prevention and a stop-if-pain rule; safeguarding rules for teen volunteers and mentors (never one-on-one, never alone with younger children).

## 3. Cross-cutting design details

### 3.1 Minimum starting age by instrument, with the readiness test

Ages are the owner's minimums. The readiness test is mine [K] and should be treated as a short teacher checklist, not a barrier.

| Instrument | Min age | Physical readiness | Cognitive readiness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Piano/keyboard | 6 | Can curve fingers, press individual keys, sit with feet supported (footstool) | Counts to 10, follows 2-step instructions, steady-beat with a partner |
| Guitar | 7 | Can press a string to the fretboard with a fingertip on a 1/4-1/2 nylon guitar; reaches fret 1 with slightly bent elbow (fit test [S]) | Follows a 3-step instruction; can match strum to beat |
| Bass | 9 | Larger hand span and longer reach, short-scale bass (about 30-inch scale (unverified)); enough finger strength for thicker strings | Can follow a chord chart or root-note pattern |
| Drums/percussion | 7 | Feet reach pedals with throne set properly; or pad work seated; can hold sticks | Keeps a steady beat for 8 bars; can follow simple pattern |
| Voice | 8 | Healthy voice, can sing comfortably in about an octave range [K] | Matches pitch in small range; reads lyrics |

Note on voice: studio sources vary from "no correct age" to 7+ and one teacher takes no young students at all [S*]; a claim that the larynx cannot execute belting until puberty is from a single blog and should be verified [S*, unverified]. Plan: no belting until adolescence, with gentle range and no strain [K].

### 3.2 Instrument sizing

| Instrument | Sizing guidance | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Guitar | 1/4: ages about 4-6, about 3'9"; 1/2: about 5-8, about 4'3"; 3/4: about 8-11, about 4'8"; full: about 12+, about 5'3" [S]. A second source gives 4-6 (1/4), 6-9 (1/2), 9-12 (3/4), 12 or about 150 cm (5 ft) full [S]. Height and arm length matter more than age. | Fraction names are not exact ratios; compare scale length (nut to saddle) [S]. Fit test: feet flat, guitar on leg, fretting hand reaches fret 1 with slightly bent elbow, can stretch frets 1 and 3 on one string; fail one check slightly and it may work for a few months, fail two and go smaller [S]. Reassess about every six months [S]. Nylon strings for very young children; steel from about 8-9 for older children who want pop or folk [S]. Never put steel strings on a nylon guitar [S]. Keep low action; tune before each practice [S]. |
| Electric guitar and bass | Thin, low-tension electric strings are easy to press but electrics are heavier [S]. Short-scale basses for ages 9-12. | Need a strap, light instrument, and an amp used at low volume |
| Piano/keyboard | Weighted 88-key digital piano or acoustic in the room (Suzuki piano programs require a piano, not a keyboard [S]; one Yamaha FAQ recommends a piano or Electone over a portable keyboard [S*]). Bench height: forearm level with the keys or black keys [S*]. Footstool for children [S*]. | Hand span: the 6-year-old in one database had a 5.8 in thumb-to-little-finger span; 7.4 in equals the width of eight keys but cannot play an octave comfortably [S]. Advocates (PASK, DS Standard) argue narrower keys reduce hand pain and children adapt quickly [S*]; those sources promote their own keyboards, so verify before purchasing. Practical response: small-hand repertoire, five-finger positions, no forced octaves for the youngest. |
| Drums | Junior kits for about 5-8 years / 90-120 cm; most kids outgrow junior kits around 9-12 [S*, retailer-guide level]. Drumeo notes comfortable 90 degrees for many drummers and says build the kit around kick, hi-hat and snare [S]; it does not address children. |
| Pad sizes | Start with 8-12 inch practice pads or a pad-based tabletop kit [K, unverified]. | Pads are the quiet option for hearing safety (Mayo audiologists recommend pad practice [S*]) |
| Voice | No instrument. Microphone on a stand or handheld at child height. | Give a stool or wedge for short children; avoid in-ear levels that are high |

### 3.3 Reading versus ear by band

| Band | Ear/imitation | Reading | Rationale |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4-5 | 100% | 0% | Attention 5-10 minutes [S]; reading letters is emerging [S] |
| 6-8 | about 70% | about 30% reading readiness | Suzuki delayed reading [S]; Kodály singing-first [S*]; songs-first modern band [S] |
| 9-12 | about 50% | about 50% | Reading shifts to comprehension by third grade [S]; Yamaha courses include aural plus reading and writing [S*] |
| 13-17 | about 40% | about 60% | Chart fluency and independence; ensemble parts |

These splits are my judgment [K]; none of the sources specifies percentages.

### 3.4 Repertoire and licensing

- Mix worship songs, hymns and general repertoire in every band. Hymns in the public domain avoid licensing questions [K]; contemporary worship songs normally require licensing for copying, projecting or recording [K] (Open question).
- Teach worship songs by simple chord progressions and melody, with keys chosen for children's ranges [K].
- Reference models: Suzuki's graded repertoire, where younger students want to play what older students play [S]; Little Kids Rock/Music Will's songs-first approach (getting students playing music they know and love within minutes) [S*]; NAfME's definition of modern band as student choice and collaborative music making [S].

### 3.5 Safety

**Hearing (drums, amps, worship bands)**
- NIOSH recommends an occupational 85 dBA average over 8 hours with a 3 dB exchange rate (every 3 dB higher halves the allowed time); OSHA's 85 dBA is an action level, not an instant damage threshold [S*, drum guide summary; S for the NIOSH figure in the JASA paper table].
- A 2019 review in the Journal of the Acoustical Society of America found no established acceptable risk for children and proposed an 8-hour limit of 80 dBA for children's recreational noise (about 2.1 dB or less of hearing loss in 99% of children after 18 years; equivalent to 75 dBA over 24 hours) [S].
- Permitted time at higher levels, using the 3 dB rule from 80 dBA over 8 hours (my arithmetic [K]): 90 dBA is about 48 minutes; 95 dBA about 15 minutes; 100 dBA about 5 minutes. A drum kit's level at a child's ear depends on kit, room and distance; I found no reliable measurement [S*]. Industry numbers range widely (for example 90-130 dB for acoustic drum sets in an opinion piece [S*]).
- Mayo Clinic audiologists suggest drummers practice with pads where possible, and take breaks (a figure of 30-60 minutes with protection then an equal break appears in a search summary) [S*]. NIDCD materials note that earmuffs and earplugs subtract their noise reduction rating from the sound level (for example NRR 22 turns 100 dB into 78 dB) and that covering ears does not protect hearing [S*]. Vendor claims of 22-25 dB for kids' earmuffs are not independent tests [S*].
- Design: electronic kits with mesh heads or pad-only for Level 0-2; earmuffs for every child on acoustic kits; sound-level meter in every drum room, with a house rule of aiming at or below 80-85 dBA average [K, my synthesis]; amps kept low.

**Posture and repetitive-strain prevention**
- In a large German contest sample (Gembris et al. 2020; 1,143 musicians aged 9-24), playing-related pain was reported by 76% overall: 71% at ages 9-13, 73% at 14-15, 80% at 16-17, 85% at 18-24. By instrument: guitar 57%, voice 59%, piano 70%, percussion 73%. Pain sites: neck/shoulder 69%, wrist 56%, back 55%. Students practicing above their instrument's median had more pain for most instruments. Limitations: self-reported, talent-selected sample, unvalidated questionnaire, regression models explained only about 4-5% of variance, and non-musical pain was the strongest predictor [S].
- Authors' recommendations: include musician health and prevention in regular lessons; teach effective practice techniques; schedule warm-ups, cool-downs and breaks; correct posture and encourage self-observation; improve communication among students, teachers and parents [S]. About 71% of those with pain told their mother and 58% their teacher; 12% did not feel taken seriously at all [S].
- Another survey summary (Ranelli et al.; 731 children aged 7-17) reports a 67% lifetime prevalence of playing-related musculoskeletal problems [S*].
- Design: five-minute warm-up and cool-down in every class; breaks at least every 10-15 minutes for under-10s [K]; "pain is a stop sign" rule; teachers trained to check posture; furniture adjustable; ask about pain in every class; cap practice loads (section 5).

**Child safeguarding (church-hosted programs)**
- The CDC guide *Preventing Child Sexual Abuse within Youth-serving Organizations* has six components: screening and selecting employees and volunteers; guidelines on interactions between individuals; monitoring behavior; ensuring safe environments; responding to inappropriate behavior, breaches in policy and allegations; training [S]. It recommends limiting one-on-one interactions "whenever possible by having at least two adults present at all times with youth" and options to make this mandatory [S]. It says there is no standard adult-to-youth ratio and to consider age, risk of the activity and location, and warns that adults chatting in a corner are not monitoring [S]. It recommends open, visible spaces, windows in doors, and policies on bullying and youth-to-youth abuse; criminal background checks are important but not enough on their own [S].
- Church-industry guidance (Church Mutual, GuideOne, church leadership sites; snippets only) matches: two unrelated adults, background checks, check-in/check-out, bathroom supervision, waiting periods, reporting, and including texting and online contact in the policy [S*]. These are blogs and templates, not binding standards; local law and the insurer control.
- Design for Gamo: two screened adults in every class and supervised practice session; no closed-door individual lessons; classrooms with glass doors or windows; written child-safety policy and code of conduct; screening and training for staff and volunteers before contact; named-adult check-in and check-out; communication with students only through the parent's email; no private social-media messages; photo/video consent; reporting procedure that follows state law. (Gamo has parents on site, which helps, but parents are not screened staff; do not count them as the second screened adult.) [K, subject to counsel review]

### 3.6 Instrument-specific differences in teaching

- **Piano:** early success with five-finger melodies; hand shape and wrist freedom; footstool; pedals later (feet cannot reach at 6-8).
- **Guitar:** pain on fingertips in weeks 2-4 is normal (callus building) [K]; start with Em, G, C, D, which use only the first three frets [S]; nylon first.
- **Bass:** root notes, two-finger alternation, and feeling the groove with a drummer; best taught in rhythm-section pairs [K].
- **Drums:** pad grip and rudiments first; kit patterns after the child can play steady time; hearing protection mandatory on acoustic kits.
- **Voice:** posture, breath and healthy tone; unison first; rounds and two-part singing; microphone technique; no belting for children [K].

## 4. Level ladder, Level 0-4 per instrument

These are Gamo-defined levels. Times assume weekly attendance of at least 80% of classes and the practice plan in section 5; the figures are my estimates (unverified) [K]. A rough external map (also my judgment, not a claim of equivalence): Level 1 near an ABRSM Prep Test or RCM Preparatory/Introductory stage [S][S*]; Level 3 near ABRSM Grade 1 or RCM Level 1; Level 4 near Grade 2 or RCM Level 2. One tutor source says Grade 1 typically needs one to two years of consistent learning and ABRSM suggests the Prep Test after about six to nine months [S*, unverified].

**Typical elapsed time per level (single beginner, ages 6-12):** Level 0: 8-12 weeks; Level 1: 4-6 months; Level 2: 6-9 months; Level 3: 9-12 months; Level 4: 9-12 months. Cumulative to Level 4: about 3-3.5 years (unverified). Teens starting from zero: about 20-30% faster (unverified).

### Piano / keyboard (start 6)

| Level | Skills | Typical time |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Posture, finger numbers, black-key groups, find C, steady beat; echo 3-5 note patterns by ear; each hand separately in C five-finger | 8-12 weeks |
| 1 | Hands together in five-finger C and G; quarter/half/whole notes; note names in one position; plays 3 songs from memory by ear and imitation; left-hand single notes | 4-6 months after L0 |
| 2 | C, G, F five-finger scales; reads treble and bass in position; eighth notes; dynamics; plays I-IV-V in C with root-position triads and accompanies a three-chord song | 6-9 months |
| 3 | One-octave major scales in 3-4 keys; key signatures to 2 sharps/flats; triads and inversions; reads a lead sheet with chord symbols; pedal basics; 2 contrasting pieces plus 1 worship accompaniment | 9-12 months |
| 4 | Two-octave scales and arpeggios hands together in 4 keys; syncopation; sight-reads simple pieces; reads and plays a hymn in four parts at an elementary level; accompanies a congregation song with a band | 9-12 months |

### Guitar (start 7)

| Level | Skills | Typical time |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Hold and sit correctly; name strings; pluck open strings; strum a steady beat; tune with a tuner; one- and two-finger songs | 8-12 weeks |
| 1 | Em, Am, D (or simplified), G; four-chord change at slow tempo; first melody on one string (reading tab or notes) | 4-6 months |
| 2 | Open chords C, G, D, Em, Am, A; strumming patterns; reads simple melody; plays a worship song at tempo with a metronome | 6-9 months |
| 3 | E, Dm, F (small-barre or simplified), capo use; fingerpicking pattern; pentatonic scale, riffs; plays in a small band | 9-12 months |
| 4 | Barre chords introduced; major and minor scales in first positions; reads chord charts and tab; lead and rhythm roles; simple improvisation | 9-12 months |

### Bass (start 9)

| Level | Skills | Typical time |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Posture; strap height; name strings; pluck with two fingers alternately; keep a steady quarter-note groove with a drummer | 8-12 weeks |
| 1 | Root-note lines on three chords; open strings and first-position notes; reads simple rhythms and tab | 4-6 months |
| 2 | Major scale in first position; roots and fifths; locks with kick drum; plays a worship song | 6-9 months |
| 3 | Walking and syncopated patterns; chord tones; reads bass charts; basic fills | 9-12 months |
| 4 | Pentatonic and major/minor scale shapes; plays in different keys; follows lead sheets; supports a full band | 9-12 months |

| Level | Skills | Typical time |

| 4 | Shuffle, syncopation, fills in context; reads simple charts; dynamics and ending cues; leads tempo in a band | 9-12 months |

| Level | Skills | Typical time |
|---|---|---|

| 1 | Sings two songs in key within a comfortable octave; keeps the beat; follows a conductor or leader | 4-6 months |
| 2 | Rounds and echo harmony; diction and phrasing; handheld microphone basics | 6-9 months |
| 3 | Two-part harmony; sings with accompaniment; reads simple melody with solfege or numbers; leads a verse of a worship song | 9-12 months |
| 4 | Sings in a vocal trio or ensemble; independent harmony; dynamics; leads a song with a band | 9-12 months |

## 5. Recommended weekly structure and practice guidance

**One visit per week, one family trip:**
1. Class (45-60 minutes, section 2).
2. Break (10 minutes): snack, restroom, movement.
3. Supervised practice session (about 60 minutes, two screened adults in the room or within clear sight).
4. Home practice during the week.

**Supervised practice session design (60 minutes):**

| Minutes | Ages 6-8 | Ages 9-12 | Ages 13-17 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0-5 | Settle and check assignment | Settle and check assignment | Set goals, tuning |
| 5-20 | Practice block 1 with practice partner (15 min) | Block 1 (20-25 min) | Block 1 (25 min) |
| 20-30 | Movement or listening break | Break and peer sharing | Break |
| 30-45 | Block 2 (15 min), different task | Block 2 (20 min) | Ensemble or self-directed (25 min) |
| 45-55 | Ear-training game or listening | Ear training or composition mini-session | Ear training or technique |
| 55-60 | Practice-card update and tidy | Log update | Log update |

Parent corner (10 minutes at start or end of the session): one tip card for parents on how to practice, using the Suzuki principle of calling attention to one point at a time [S]. A parent may also learn the first few notes on the instrument, as Suzuki programs encourage [S].

**Home practice minutes by age (accepted guidance is informal):**

| Source | Guidance |
|---|---|
| Teacher blog [S*, unverified] | Ages 4-6: 5-10 minutes; 6-8: 15-20 minutes; 9-12: 30-40 minutes; count repetitions for younger children |
| Another teacher [S*] | 10-15 minutes for a beginner, building to 20; a 7-year-old plays a song 7 times |
| Suzuki [S] | Two or three minutes, four or five times a day for a beginner; grows with concentration; daily listening is key |
| Forum rule [S*] | Practice equal to lesson length each day (not recommended for young children) |

**Gamo recommendation [K]:**

| Band | Home days | Minutes per day | Weekly total including the supervised hour |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4-5 | daily listening | 5 | not applicable |
| 6-8 | 5 | 10-15 (two chunks) | about 2 hours |
| 9-12 | 4-5 | 15-25 | about 2.5-3 hours |
| 13-17 | 4-5 | 20-40 | about 3-4 hours plus optional ensemble |

For comparison, the German contest sample (talented young musicians) practiced a mean of 7 hours 18 minutes per week and those over 21 hours reported 94% pain [S]. Gamo's loads are intentionally modest; frequency and quality matter more than length.

## 7. Trade-offs when constrained

Short, as the owner asked ("ideal first"). Pick these only when the ideal is blocked.

- **No room or budget for sized instruments:** use ukuleles or 1/2 nylon guitars shared by size band; do not give young children a full-size guitar.
- **No two screened adults available:** do not run the session; pair a screened volunteer with parents present, and delay opening that class.
- **Only 30-minute slots:** run semi-private 30-minute lessons (two students) and reduce the supervised practice to 30 minutes [K].
- **No separate drum room:** pad-only classes and earmuffs for any acoustic kit use; measure the neighboring rooms.
- **Mixed ages in one class:** accept 6-8 and 9-12 together only if the ladder level is the same; keep teens in a separate cohort.
- **Fewer teachers:** keep group size at the low end of each range and use older students as listening partners, never as unsupervised teachers.
- **Parents cannot attend:** require a named adult for pick-up and a weekly email summary; switch the parent role to a "home listener".

## 8. Implications for Gamo Academy

1. **Offer one weekly visit: class plus supervised practice.** It removes the family's second drive and gives each child about 2 hours of structured time, with home practice limited to short chunks.
2. **Design by age band, not just by instrument.** Four cohorts (Foundations 4-5 if approved, 6-8, 9-12, 13-17) so friendships form; separate teen beginners from child beginners.
3. **Make the practice hour a product of design.** Two instrument blocks, break, ear-training game, parent corner; train supervisors to coach one point at a time [S].
4. **Adopt the Level 0-4 ladder with in-class checkpoints, ABRSM/RCM/Trinity as optional.** The goal is competence and long-term identity as a musician, not exam completion [S].
5. **Build a hearing-safety program from day one:** sound-level meter, earmuffs, pad or electronic drum practice, volume rules. Cheap to do and hard to retrofit.
6. **Build a child-safeguarding program from day one,** following the CDC youth-serving organization components and counsel; church-hosted spaces have their own policies, and Gamo's must be at least as strict.
7. **Buy a sized instrument fleet and an adjustable furniture set** (footstools, adjustable benches, drum thrones) before the first class.
8. **Use worship songs, hymns and general repertoire in every cohort, with age-appropriate arrangements,** and clarify licensing before any recording or performance outside the class.
9. **Adopt non-commercial retention levers:** autonomy, competence, relatedness. Avoid contingent reward systems (the evidence says they can undermine motivation [S]); make badges informational ("you can now play G-C-D") rather than prizes. This also fits the mission: success is educational and religious impact, not conversion of customers.
10. **Record the decisions** in `docs/DECISIONS.md` after the owner answers the questions below. Ladder and curriculum names will feed form fields and emails later (levels become data); keep them stable.
