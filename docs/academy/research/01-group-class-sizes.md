# Gamo Academy research 01: group class sizes

Date: 2026-11-16. Scope: how big a group class should be, per instrument and age band, and when group beats (or loses to) private lessons. Per the owner's instruction this states the **ideal first** and puts constraints last.

**Evidence labels.** **[S]** = read in a source this session (the number in brackets points to "Sources" at the end). **[S-excerpt]** = seen only in a search-result excerpt of the page because a direct fetch failed; treat as lower confidence. **[K]** = prior knowledge or my judgment, not verified. **(unverified)** marks numbers I could not trace to a source. Nothing below is a quote unless in quotation marks.

## Summary

- **No authoritative body publishes an "ideal group size" per instrument.** I found none from NAfME, MTNA, Suzuki Association, RCM or ABRSM that gives a number for a studio or hosted-site class. The numbers in this document are a synthesis of practice sources plus my judgment [K], and they are labeled that way. [S]
- **Best-supported size range for beginners: 3 to 8 students per teacher.** Practice sources cluster there: Suzuki Association school guidance says 5-10 and "smaller if the students are younger" [15, S-excerpt]; Yamaha Music School Canada runs 4-10 with a parent present [3]; the only controlled piano study used 3 per group [1]; UK law since 1988 allowed charging for groups of up to 4 [13].
- **Size itself has almost no experimental evidence.** One 1980 study (Jackson, reported in [1]) varied piano class size (2, 4, 6, 8) across four age groups and found no significant difference, but samples were 8-12 per age group. [S, secondhand]
- **Group vs private, for beginners: roughly equal achievement, group better at sight-reading and social outcomes, private better at technique correction.** Chiu's review of earlier studies says group results were "typically equal to or slightly superior"; most had 24 or fewer participants [1]. Teachers surveyed in Sweden say individual lessons are better for technical skill and group for social skill [10].
- **Age matters at 5 vs 6-7.** In Chiu's 24-week study (45 children, ages 5-7), 5-year-olds did better in group; 6- and 7-year-olds did better privately; there was no overall difference [2]. Small sample, one site, so a signal, not a rule.
- **The "individual attention per minute" budget is the binding constraint, and assistants fix it.** With one teacher, about 3 minutes of direct attention per student per class means roughly 6 students in 45 minutes [K, arithmetic below]. A second adult roughly doubles capacity. The hour of supervised practice after class is where Gamo can give each student real one-to-one minutes.
- **Retention evidence is thin.** UK whole-class programs report continuation "from 15% to 100%" depending on how it is counted, with "just under a third" continuing nationally [11]. No controlled study compares retention between group and private piano that I could find.
- **Recommended Gamo default (ideal):** small same-level groups of 4-6 (3 minimum), one instrument per student, a screened adult assistant for any group over 4 under age 10, a parent or family member present for ages 5-6, 40-60% of class time hands-on, plus 3-5 minutes of individual check-in per student inside the practice hour. Full table in section 9.

## 1. How to read the numbers: three constraints that set group size

1. **Attention budget [K].** Per-student direct teacher time per class = (class minutes x share of time spent giving individual feedback) / students. At 45 minutes and 40% of the class on rotating individual feedback (18 minutes), 4 students get 4.5 minutes each, 6 get 3, 8 get 2.25. I set about 3 minutes as the practical floor for one teacher; an assistant adds a second feedback stream.
2. **Instrument access [S, K].** NAfME's Opportunity-to-Learn standard for guitar/keyboard classes says there should be "a sufficient number of guitars/keyboards or other harmonizing instruments such that all students can be simultaneously engaged in making music" [7]. That is a school standard, but it is the clearest authoritative statement: one instrument per student, no waiting. Gamo's ideal follows it.
3. **Noise and room [S, K].** Piano-lab vendors plan 5 x 5 ft per workstation and 4 ft walking space; a vendor minimum room for 8 students is 24 x 19 ft [4] (commercial source, may be generous). Headphones and a lab controller make piano labs workable; drums and acoustic guitars need either quiet pads/headphone amps or separate rooms [K].

Note on schools: sources quoting 20-38 students for guitar classes [28] or "absolutely no more than 25-30" [29] describe daily school classes. They are not a model for a 30-60 minute weekly hosted class, and Gamo should not use them as a benchmark.

## 2. Piano and keyboard (group piano or keyboard lab, one instrument per student, headphones)

**Ideal / range [K, informed by S]:** ideal 4-6; minimum 3; maximum 8 with an assistant (10 for adults and teens with a lab controller).

**Reasoning.**
- Terminology: Pike's group-piano book distinguishes a "group" (about 3-10) from a "class" (more than 10) [8, S-excerpt via bookseller summary]. Wikipedia's summary says group piano typically has "between 3 and 16 students" [6]. Both are descriptive, not prescriptive.
- Chiu's controlled study used **3 students per teacher, 50 minutes plus 10 minutes of parent discussion**, same-age groups, same peers all 24 weeks [1].
- Rogers (1974, via [1]) used pairs for 45 minutes plus a 60-minute class of 8 per week; Jackson (1980, via [1]) found no difference between 2/4 and 6/8 [1].
- Lab practice: buy student pianos in multiples of four so the class splits into duets or quartets; a lab conferencing system (broadcast one student, mute the room, work groups) is "essential" in that author's view [5]. Practitioners say 8-12 or more needs a controller [search excerpt, unverified origin].
- A working teacher-owner running 8 digital pianos per classroom uses a 3-student "20/20/20" format: 20 minutes private, 20 minutes theory lab, 20 minutes ensemble [9].

**Evidence vs folklore.**
- Evidence: small controlled studies show group is not worse for beginners [1]. Dropout research argues piano dropout differs from band/orchestra because private piano lacks a built-in social group [35, S-excerpt].
- Folklore: claims like "triple or quadruple the skills" of private students come from a school owner's blog with no data [32]. "Group piano needs 12+ to be economical" is a business argument, not a pedagogy finding [K].

**Class format [K, shaped by 1, 5, 9] (45 minutes, 4-6 students):**

| Minutes | Activity | Who |
|---|---|---|
| 0-5 | Pulse, posture, warm-up in unison | Teacher leads, all play |
| 5-12 | One new idea at the keyboard, echo and call-response | Teacher, max 7 minutes continuous talk |
| 12-30 | Hands-on in pairs or trios, headphones, teacher and assistant rotate 2-3 minutes per student | Students, individual feedback |
| 30-40 | Peer performance and a duet or ensemble piece | Students listen, give one specific comment |
| 40-45 | Practice plan for the supervised hour | Teacher |

Teacher talk about 25%, hands-on at least 60%. Ofsted's finding on school music lessons that "there was not enough music in music lessons" and "too much use was made of verbal communication" is the warning behind this [12].

## 3. Guitar (acoustic, electric, class guitar)

**Ideal / range [K]:** ideal 4-6; minimum 3; maximum 8 with an assistant (10 for adults). Electric: stay at the low end unless every player has a headphone amp or a modeler.

**Reasoning.**
- Guitar needs physical correction (hand shape, fretting pressure, posture) that a teacher must give one student at a time. A Swedish study of 4-5-student groups: teachers said individual lessons correct technique faster; one teacher would not correct someone in front of peers and "lets inaccuracy slip through in favour of keeping the music going" [10]. That is the main limit on group size.
- Tuning, string changes and sore fingertips are assistant tasks; a guitar class of more than 4 beginners will burn the first 10 minutes on tuning without help [K].
- Class guitar in schools is run on 20-38 students daily [28, 29]. The Aaron Shearer Foundation suggests an assistant (an advanced student or nearby college guitar major) for larger groups, and a daily routine of about 25% group warm-up without music, 60% group study with music, 15% individual practice [29]. That time split is reusable at smaller sizes.
- NAfME's standard sets quality markers for Gamo to borrow: multi-level class options, ability-based scheduling [7].

**Evidence vs folklore.** I found no controlled study on guitar class size. A South African study (van der Merwe, North-West University) found that cooperative, peer-based practice reduced anxiety in a guitar class of about 150 adult education students [30]; it supports peer practice, not any size. The claim that guitar students "continue playing after high school more than any other group except voice students" is a teacher's statement in a NAfME blog [28], not research.

**Class format [K] (45-60 minutes, 4-6 students):** 5 min tuning and posture check (assistant helps), 10 min new chord or riff by echo, 20 min play-along with backing track (music keeps going; the teacher moves behind each student), 10 min pairs teach each other, 5 min peer performance. Repetition with small changes ("same thing, but they don't perceive it as the same") keeps everyone in the activity [10].

## 4. Bass guitar

**Ideal / range [K]:** ideal 3-4 in a bass-only class; maximum 6. Better: teach bass inside a **rhythm-section group** (bass, drums, keys/guitar, 6-8 total) where the bassist's job is audible. Start age about 10 or when hands span the fretboard comfortably (unverified).

**Reasoning.** Bass is rarely studied on its own. Low-frequency noise carries through walls, a good bass amp is expensive, and the instrument's purpose (locking with the drums) needs other players [K]. Group of 3-4 gives each player a headphone amp, an amp plugged into the room mix, and 4+ minutes of individual attention. No source I read gives a bass-specific size.

**Format [K]:** 10 min pulse and note-length with backing track; 15 min groove built with a drummer or a drum loop; 10 min rotation one-on-one with the assistant (finger placement, muting); 10 min play-along.

## 5. Drums and percussion

**Ideal / range [K]:**
- **Pad ensemble / rudiment class** (one practice pad and sticks per student): ideal 4-6, maximum 8 with an assistant.
- **Drum kit class** (kits or e-kits): ideal 3-4 with one kit and 2-3 pad stations on rotation; maximum 4 per kit. A teacher alone should not run more than one kit station plus pads.
- **Hand percussion / body percussion for ages 5-6:** ideal 6-8, maximum 10 with parent present (see section 8).

**Reasoning.** Pads are quiet, cheap and equal for everyone, so they remove the main limit (instrument access). A kit is loud and cannot be shared at the same moment. Orff Schulwerk-style instrument play is a core group activity where "participation by all, each at his or her own level" is the stated aim [27]; I found no number from AOSA [S].

**Evidence vs folklore.** I found no study on drum class size. The commercial listings I read show small groups, a 45-minute class, a 10-week cycle, ages 7+ for a drum-kit course, and a final performance for peers [33]. Treat as a market example, not evidence. The UK whole-class programs discussed in [14] included African drumming and steel pans alongside traditional instruments, and the letter reports more pupils wanting to continue than with small groups of pre-selected pupils (secondhand; see section 7).

**Format [K] (45 min):** 5 min grip and posture; 10 min rudiment of the day on pads (everyone plays, teacher circulates); 15 min rotation: one student at the kit with the teacher, others on pads with the assistant or a practice card; 10 min groove play-along with a loop; 5 min peer performance.

## 6. Voice (small vocal groups vs choirs; vocal health and age limits)

**Ideal / range [K]:**
- **Voice technique group** (including worship vocals, microphone work): ideal 4-6, maximum 8.
- **Children's singing group / choir:** ideal 12-16, maximum about 24 with two adults (unverified; I found no source with a number).
- **Ages 5-7:** a group of 6-8 with movement and singing games, led from the front, not a harmony choir.

**Reasoning.**
- Each singer needs individual listening; with a mic, each singer should have turns solo. At 6, a 45-minute class allows 2-3 minutes of solo each [K].
- Child choir singers had fewer voice disorders than non-singers in a Barcelona cohort (1,495 children aged 8-14; 15.6% vs 32.4%); the authors cite voice training, regular voice checks and parent and child education as possible protective factors, but it is observational and the choirs may have selected better voices [17, S]. The singers sang about 7.5 hours per week on average [17].
- A UK school study (58 children, ages 5-7; no control group) found singing competence improved over six months with visiting professional singers every two weeks and teachers between visits [18]. Years 1 and 2 had 29 children each, which is a school class, not a Gamo group.
- Teaching vocal health: "Teach singers about puberty and how it affects the voice" is called a primary job of the director; check ranges often; use treble or non-gender-specific parts; avoid parts at the break; zero tolerance for mocking [19, S].

**Age limits and voice change [S, with caveats].** Practitioner guidance (Cooksey stages): boys unchanged at 10-11, change often 12-15 and can start at 9, lasting 18 months to three years; girls change from about 11-15 and may take up to four years, less dramatically [20, practitioner blog]. NAfME's guidance says stages are "stages of vocal development, not voice parts" and that timing varies widely (one boy may stay in a stage a year, another a month) [19]. Ranges mentioned in the sources (about two octaves for healthy 7-10-year-olds [search excerpt, unverified]) are not a Gamo standard.

**Rules for Gamo [K, from 17, 19, 20]:** group by voice stage, not only by age, from age 10 up; no forced, loud or belted singing for children (unverified recommendation; consult a children's voice specialist); screen for vocal strain after every session (no rule on hours found, so set a conservative cap of one voice class per day for under-13s); no upper age limit for adults, but warm-up and rest are mandatory.

**Evidence vs folklore.** Folklore: "choir is bad for young voices" or "choir is automatically good." The Barcelona study shows association only [17]; a 2025 study of 45 child choir singers (mean age 13) reported mild dysphonia overall and vocal complaints in half after performances (Voice Foundation 2025 abstract, search excerpt only; not listed in Sources) [S-excerpt]. Moderation and monitoring are the supported messages.

## 7. Mixed-instrument beginner ensembles

**Ideal / range [K]:** ideal 6-8 (rhythm section plus keys, guitars and voices); minimum 5; maximum 10-12 with two adults. Ages 7-9 should stay at 6-8; teenagers and adults can handle 8-12.

**Reasoning.**
- The UK Music Education Grant director's report says the best whole-class practice "is not simply a larger version of the traditional individual lesson" and advises teachers to consider "organising whole class ensemble tuition as if it were an ensemble rehearsal" [13]; the same report describes **catch-up or extension** time "as and when it is required" instead of a weekly individual lesson [13].
- The key findings of the UK Whole Class Ensemble Teaching (WCET) study: success depends on committed staff, high-quality arrangements that **differentiate** parts for different levels, frequent performances, parental support and a **seamless route** to ensembles afterward [11].
- The Education Scotland review of the El Sistema-inspired Big Noise program found "regular group work and individual tuition," differentiated parts at different levels, and small "breakaway groups" for more able learners and those with additional needs; a "high ratio of adults to children, including support staff and volunteers" helped the program focus on individual needs. No numeric ratio is given [21].
- A Swedish study found teachers keep everyone engaged by never stopping the music, adding games and role changes (such as "everyone switches seats" after each play-through) [10].

**Evidence vs folklore.** Strong practitioner agreement, weak trial evidence. Hallam's report on continuation: "just under a third" of pupils continued after whole-class tuition nationally; reported continuation across providers ranged "from 15% to 100%"; one exemplary school saw "between 50% and 70%" transition to small-group and individual lessons, and the report calls the topic "sensitive and contentious" because counting methods differ [11]. A letter from an academic to the TES says an Ofsted pilot evaluation found whole-class or large-group tuition produced "70 to 100 per cent" more pupils wanting to continue in some cases [14] (a letter, secondhand).

**Format [K] (60 minutes):** 10 min warm-up and pulse; 15 min sectionals by instrument with teacher and assistant; 20 min full ensemble on one song with differentiated parts (easy/medium/stretch); 10 min performance for peers or families; 5 min practice plan.

## 8. Early childhood and parent-and-child classes

**Ideal / range [K, informed by S]:**
- **Ages 0-3 with caregiver (music and movement):** ideal 6-8 child-and-adult pairs; maximum 10-12.
- **Ages 3-5 with caregiver present for part or all:** ideal 6-8 children; maximum 10-12 (with a second adult past 8).
- **Ages 4-6 with parent, introducing instruments (Yamaha style):** ideal 6-8; maximum 10.

**Reasoning.**
- Music Together centers set their own sizes; TCU's classes run **3-12 children, 45 minutes, caregiver required** [24]. Other Music Together centers report 5-12 and 8-12 or 10-12 (search excerpts from individual centers, unverified). Music Together stresses that "the grownups are the examples and models," and caregivers are asked to put phones away [25].
- Yamaha Music School Canada: 4-10 students, 50-55 minutes weekly; parent attendance required for ages 3-5, optional for 6-9 [3]. Hungary and Czech listings for the Junior Extension Course say 6-10 (search excerpt) [S-excerpt].
- Kindermusik levels run from 0-12 months through 5-7 years, with mixed-age classes for 0-7 and caregivers joining a portion of Level 3 classes; no size on the official page [26]. An unofficial 2010 post gives "up to 10 children with at least one caregiver per family" [S-excerpt, unverified].
- Room sizing matters: 8 children also means 8 adults [K].

**Evidence vs folklore.** The parent-participation research I found is small and qualitative: parents join mainly for expected benefits to the child; one case study found family bonding as an outcome; one dissertation found parents struggled to state their own goals and did not join in order to improve their musical parenting [search excerpts]. There is no study here linking class size to outcomes; the numbers are what established programs run.

**Parent role.** Treat the family member as the "assistant" for ages 3-6: sits beside the child, models singing and movement, and is the practice partner at home (the Suzuki "triangle" idea [K]). In Chiu's study, parents were required to join a 10-minute post-lesson discussion and about 50% watched lessons [1].

**Format [K] (30-45 minutes):** hello song and name song (3 min); movement and rhythm (10); instrument play, such as shakers, drums, bells (10); listening or quiet song (5); free play with the family, goodbye (5). Keep each activity under 5-7 minutes for under-fives [K].

## 10. Group vs private lessons: outcomes and retention

**What the studies say [S unless noted].**
- Chiu's review: group instruction "typically equal to or slightly superior" to private in elementary piano and other instruments; Rogers (1974) found group better on all aspects tested but gave unequal instruction time; Diehl (1980), Seipp (1976), Hutcherson (1955) found group better at sight-reading; Sims (1961) found group better for beginning high-school vocalists. Caveats: single teacher in many studies, most with 24 or fewer participants [1].
- Chiu's own study: no significant overall difference; age x mode interaction (group better at 5, private better at 6-7; strongest for kinesthetic response). Her explanations: attitudes toward group work, growth of self-consciousness, sociability, achievement motivation. Her teachers noted 5-year-olds followed the teacher and joined activities, while 6-7-year-olds were more easily distracted by peers and made faster progress in private lessons [1, 2].
- A 2025/26 interview study of five Sri Lankan piano teachers found both formats effective; four of five said group teaching builds teamwork, engagement and achievement more [31]. Perception data only.
- A Swedish government inquiry (2016) recommended group tuition as the new norm in municipal music schools; teacher attitudes were mixed and master's-level studies said individual is better for technique and group for social skills [10].

**Retention.**
- No controlled comparison found. A 2017 dropout study (Erudit) argues piano dropout needs its own research because private piano lacks a group, and band/orchestra findings do not transfer [35, S-excerpt].
- Practitioner claim: a group-piano school owner says group offerings "will definitely improve student retention" and that converting from private to group cost her 40% of students at first [9]. One practitioner, not a study.
- Whole-class UK programs: continuation low and hard to measure (see section 7) [11].

**When group is better [K, supported by 1, 9, 10, 11].** Beginners; rhythm, ensemble and sight-reading; singing and rhythm-section instruments; motivation and belonging; families who need access at low cost; age 5; performance anxiety.

**When group is worse [K, supported by 1, 10].** Technique problems that need hands-on correction (guitar hand position, drum grip and posture, voice strain); wide level gaps; ages 6-7 who are easily distracted by peers (Chiu's observation); students who are shy to the point of not engaging (Merriam says about 5% cannot adapt; commercial claim, unverified) [32]; injury; audition or exam preparation; advanced repertoire.

**Best practice [K]:** group-first with a built-in individual component. Lee's models range from "one monthly group class plus weekly private" to "20/20/20 with 3 students" [9]. Hallam's report supports short "catch-up or extension" individual time as needed, instead of automatic weekly private lessons [13]. For Gamo, the supervised practice hour is the natural place for this (see section 12).

## 11. Teaching assistants and older peer mentors

**What sources say [S].**
- Education Scotland's review of a Sistema-inspired program credits support staff who "know the children well" and a "high ratio of adults to children, including support staff and volunteers" for focusing on individual needs; it also found support staff and volunteers "not always clear of their role at first" and recommends robust induction [21].
- The Aaron Shearer Foundation suggests an assistant (advanced student or nearby college guitar major) for classes of more than 25-30 [29].
- Alfred's peer-mentoring article: structure peer mentoring as an intentional relationship; spend about 5-7 minutes per class on preparing mentors (brainstorm qualities, identify strengths, set shared goals); check in over a set learning cycle; no ratios given [22].
- Near-peer mentorship reports (UNCG, a 2020 community-program case study, Sistema-style "peer helpers") are positive but are program reports, not trials; one author notes children often "don't understand what helping their peer students means," so mentors need preparation [23, S-excerpt].
- I found **no ratio from any research source**. The ratios below are my judgment.

**Recommended ratios [K].**
- Screened adult assistant: 1 per 4-6 students under age 10; 1 per 6-8 ages 10-13; 1 per 8-10 for 14 and up. Adults count for safeguarding.
- Older peer mentors (age 14-17 or advanced students): 1 mentor per 2-3 mentees, as a **supplement**, never counted as the adult ratio and never alone with a student behind a closed door. Mentors get training (the 5-7 minute routine above plus a role card), a clear scope (tuning, counting, posture reminders, listening; not correcting technique), and recognition.
- Parents at ages 3-6: the parent is the assistant for their child only, not for the group.

**What assistants do:** tune, set up, rotate through individual check-ins, watch hand shape, run the practice-hour plan, and give the lead teacher time for the next group.

## 12. Blending a small group with individual attention

Options, ranked for Gamo's class-plus-supervised-practice model [K, with 9, 13, 29]:

1. **Inside the class (rotation).** Teacher or assistant visits each student for 2-3 minutes during hands-on time. With 4-6 students and 18 minutes of rotation, that is 3-4.5 minutes each. [arithmetic, K]
2. **Inside the practice hour (best fit).** After class, a supervised practice hour of 60 minutes: 10 min warm-up with the day's plan card, two 20-minute practice blocks (children under 8 break each into 10-minute tasks), a 5-minute "play for a friend" at the end. The teacher or assistant takes each student for a **3-5 minute check-in** during the blocks, so 6 students use 18-30 minutes of adult time and each student gets a mini private lesson every week. This is conceptually Lee's "20/20/20" idea (individual, lab, ensemble) spread across the visit [9].
3. **Pairs and trios.** Students teach each other one skill for 5 minutes; Chiu's group of 3 allowed this [1].
4. **Periodic individual checks.** Every 6-8 weeks, a 10-minute one-on-one progress check per student (no fee consequence; Gamo's mission rules apply) [K].
5. **Catch-up and extension slots.** Short individual time "as and when it is required" rather than automatic private lessons [13].

## 13. What changes by age [K unless marked]

| Age | What changes | Group design response |
|---|---|---|
| **5-6** | Short attention span; learns through movement, songs and the parent; fine motor control still developing; in Chiu's study, 5-year-olds did better in group, 6-year-olds started to prefer private [2] | Smaller groups (4-6 piano, 6-8 percussion/singing); parent beside the child; 20-30 minute class blocks with a change every 5-7 minutes; no harmony singing; no guitar or bass; no continuous 60 minutes of practice |
| **7-9** | Can read, work in pairs and follow a plan; more peer-aware and easily distracted [1]; hands still small | Pairs and trios; same-age, same-level grouping; instruments in right sizes; assistant for any group over 4-6; gentle peer performance |
| **10-13** | Peer opinion matters more; voice change begins for some (boys from about 9-12, girls 11+) [19, 20]; strongest social benefit | Groups of 5-8; ensembles and bands; peer-mentor training for the oldest; check ranges often; avoid public embarrassment during voice change |
| **14-17** | Self-conscious about mistakes; can mentor; schedules compete (school, work) | Choice of repertoire; band format; 6-10 per group; mentor roles with adult oversight; private check-ins available on request |
| **Adults** | Self-directed, anxious about being "behind"; limited practice time; family members who drive may join | 6-10 per group; adult-only classes for beginners; lab or guitar group works the same; (see `03-adult-learners-and-retention.md` for adult-specific retention research) |

## 14. Implications for Gamo Academy

1. **Use the ideal as the default design, not the ceiling.** Same-level groups of 4-6, one instrument per student, minimum 3. These numbers are our judgment informed by practice sources; we should say so internally and not present them as research results.
2. **Make the supervised practice hour the individual-attention engine.** A 3-5 minute check-in per student per week by a teacher or assistant is the single most useful blend of group and private. It needs adults, so staffing assistants is part of the pedagogy, not an extra.
3. **Staff by adult ratio, not by headcount alone.** Plan screened adult assistants as in section 11; peer mentors are a training and community feature that supplements, not replaces, adults. Church and school hosts will have their own safeguarding rules; follow the stricter one [K].
4. **Instruments and rooms.** One keyboard per student with headphones and a lab controller (or a plan to run without); guitars and pads loaned on site so the family member driving does not carry gear; separate or acoustically treated space for kit drumming and amps. A church fellowship hall will not do for 3 simultaneous classes without room dividers or headphones [K].
5. **Do not start younger instruments too early.** No guitar or bass group at 5-6; use ukulele, Orff-style instruments, piano and singing instead [K].
6. **Voice program: protect young voices.** Group by voice stage from 10 up, track vocal strain, avoid forced loud singing for children, and have a voice-change policy for teens and a policy on how many voice classes a child may take per day (my cap of one is unverified).
7. **Run honest measurement.** Evidence on size and retention is thin. Record, from day one and without personal data in public, minutes of individual attention per student, attendance, and who continues at 3, 6 and 12 months. That would give Gamo better evidence than most published sources. Do not publish retention or outcome claims until Gamo has its own data.
8. **Mission fit.** Smaller groups plus assistants cost more per student than large classes. Because Academy is a mission program, the owner decides funding and fees; this document does not propose any fee, tier or pricing. Per `docs/GAMO_NONPROFIT_MODEL.md`, any payment model remains a proposal until the owner approves it.

## 15. Trade-offs when constrained (short)

If rooms, instruments or adults fall short, reduce in this order [K]:

1. **Keep one instrument per student; shrink the group before sharing instruments.** Sharing a keyboard or guitar cuts hands-on time in half.
2. **Hold the 3-minute individual attention floor.** If there is no assistant, cap at 6 (4 for guitar and drums). If there is one, 8 is workable for piano, guitar pads and adult groups.
3. **Drop the kit before the pads.** Pads give everyone sticks in hand; a shared kit is a waiting line.
4. **Use pairs and trios as the unit.** A class of 8 that works as four pairs behaves like a smaller class.
5. **Use mixed-level classes only with differentiated parts** (easy/medium/stretch), as in WCET and Big Noise [11, 21].
6. **Keep parents at 5-6.** The family member is a free assistant.
7. **If the whole-class size must go up (for example 12-20 for an ensemble or choir), add adults, shorten talk to under 20%, and keep sectionals.** Do not respond by running 20-minute lectures.
8. **Combine instruments into ensemble groups** to make a half-room class workable (for example, a worship band of 8).
